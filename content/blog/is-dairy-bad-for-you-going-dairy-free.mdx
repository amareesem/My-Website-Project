<p><strong>Dairy isn’t harmful for everyone, but many people feel better without it. About two-thirds of adults worldwide don’t fully digest lactose.</strong> Cheese, butter, and cream are major sources of saturated fat, sodium, and hidden calories, and the brain-protective MIND diet limits cheese and butter. You also don’t need dairy for strong bones: calcium from greens like kale and bok choy is absorbed better than calcium from milk. A full dairy break is the simplest way to learn how your body responds.</p>
<p>I know this one feels big. First I took your cookies, then your crackers, and now your cheese? Take a deep breath. I’m not taking anything away. I’m helping you find out how your body feels without a food group many people react to, and showing you how to cover every nutrient dairy used to bring.</p>
<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>About two-thirds of adults worldwide have a reduced ability to digest lactose, the sugar in milk.</li>
<li>Cheese has about 110 calories per ounce, and it’s easy to eat three or four ounces without noticing.</li>
<li>The MIND diet limits cheese to less than one serving a week and butter to less than a tablespoon a day.</li>
<li>In large studies, drinking more milk didn’t protect against hip fractures.</li>
<li>You absorb about 32% of the calcium in milk, versus about 49 to 61% from kale, bok choy, and broccoli.</li>
<li>Plan your calcium, check medication timing, and talk with your physician if you have bone loss.</li>
</ul>
<h2>One Cheese Plate, Three Battles</h2>
<h3>Dairy and weight gain</h3>
<p>The research on milk itself and weight is mixed. But the way most of us eat dairy isn’t. Cheese packs about 110 calories per ounce, and it disappears fast: melted on top, sprinkled on salads, nibbled while cooking. Add butter, cream, flavored creamers, and ice cream, and dairy can quietly add hundreds of calories a day. Remove it, replace it with whole foods, and many people find the scale moves more easily.</p>
<h3>Dairy, heart health, and bones</h3>
<p>Full-fat cheese, butter, cream, and ice cream are some of the biggest sources of saturated fat in the American diet, and saturated fat, especially from butter, raises LDL (“bad”) cholesterol. Cheese is also a top source of sodium.</p>
<p>And the bone story isn’t what we were told. In large studies following tens of thousands of adults, <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20949604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">drinking more milk did not protect against hip fractures</a> (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25352269/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">see also this <em>BMJ</em> cohort</a>). You need calcium for strong bones. You just don’t need to get it from a cow.</p>
<h3>Dairy and brain health</h3>
<p>The <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25681666/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">MIND diet</a>, built around brain-health research, has foods to <em>limit</em>, and two of them are cheese (less than one serving a week) and butter (less than a tablespoon a day). In the <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12580703/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Chicago Health and Aging Project</a>, older adults who ate the most saturated fat had about twice the risk of Alzheimer’s as those who ate the least. What clogs your arteries isn’t good for the blood vessels in your brain, either.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>A word about the evidence:</strong> Dairy research is mixed. <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25420418/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Some studies link fermented dairy</a> like plain yogurt to a lower risk of type 2 diabetes. So why take a break from all dairy? Because many people react without realizing it, with bloating, digestive upset, or skin flare-ups, and the only way to know is to remove it completely and see how you feel. If you and your physician later test a food back in, you’ll know exactly how your body responds.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>How Dairy Can Work Against You</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lactose intolerance is the norm.</strong> About two-thirds of adults worldwide have a reduced ability to digest lactose. That’s the normal setting for mammals, most of which lose the ability after weaning. Gassy or bloated after dairy? This may be why.</li>
<li><strong>Milk proteins.</strong> Some people who aren’t lactose intolerant still feel better without dairy: fewer digestive complaints, clearer skin, less joint achiness. An elimination is how you find out.</li>
<li><strong>Acne.</strong> In a <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29778512/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">review of studies of more than 70,000 people</a>, higher dairy intake, especially milk, was linked to a higher risk of acne.</li>
<li><strong>IGF-1.</strong> Drinking milk raises IGF-1, a growth signal. In adults, higher IGF-1 has been <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361446/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">linked in observational studies to a higher risk of some cancers</a>, including prostate cancer. The research isn’t settled.</li>
<li><strong>What’s in conventional milk.</strong> Some U.S. dairies still use rBST, a synthetic growth hormone that isn’t allowed in organic dairy or approved in Canada or the EU. Milk from pregnant cows also naturally contains small amounts of estrogen and progesterone; whether those amounts matter for people is still being studied.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>The honest math:</strong> Greens are absorbed better, but milk starts with more calcium, so you need a generous portion. About a cup of cooked kale or bok choy delivers roughly the same <em>absorbed</em> calcium as a glass of milk. Most adults need 1,000 to 1,200 mg of calcium a day.</p>
<p><strong>Best non-dairy calcium sources:</strong> cooked collards, kale, turnip greens, and bok choy; broccoli; calcium-set tofu (look for “calcium sulfate”); sesame seeds and tahini; almonds and almond butter; figs and oranges; and calcium-fortified unsweetened plant milks. Learn to cook them in our guide to <a href="/blog/calcium-without-dairy-cooked-leafy-greens">calcium without dairy</a>.</p>
<h2>How to Go Dairy-Free: Your Plan</h2>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Clear out</strong> all milk (including lactose-free), cheese, yogurt and kefir, butter, ice cream, and cream cheese, sour cream, and cottage cheese.</li>
<li><strong>Watch for hidden dairy:</strong> casein, whey, lactose, and milk solids show up in bread, salad dressings, protein powders, “non-dairy” creamers, processed meats, some dark chocolate, and some supplements. A food-scanning app like Bobby Approved helps.</li>
<li><strong>Try 2 or 3 unsweetened, calcium-fortified plant milks</strong> to find your favorite.</li>
<li><strong>Eat at least two calcium-rich plant foods every day</strong> (spinach doesn’t count).</li>
<li><strong>Keep a simple journal</strong> of your digestion, skin, energy, and sinuses.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Safety check:</strong> If you have osteoporosis or osteopenia, or take a bone-building medication, talk with your physician first to plan calcium and vitamin D, and ask about checking your vitamin D level. If you take levothyroxine, calcium (including fortified plant milks, calcium-set tofu, and supplements) can block its absorption: take your thyroid pill on an empty stomach and keep calcium at least 4 hours away. <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10838651/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Calcium affects levothyroxine absorption</a>, and similar timing issues apply to some antibiotics and osteoporosis medications, so ask your pharmacist. If you’re pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning a child’s diet, check with your physician first.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Best Dairy-Free Milk Alternatives</h2>
<p>Choose <strong>unsweetened</strong> and <strong>fortified with calcium and vitamin D</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Almond milk:</strong> low calorie, neutral flavor, very little protein</li>
<li><strong>Coconut milk beverage:</strong> rich and creamy, great in coffee</li>
<li><strong>Oat milk:</strong> naturally sweet and froths well, a bit higher in carbs</li>
<li><strong>Hemp milk:</strong> creamy, with plant omega-3s</li>
<li><strong>Organic soy milk:</strong> closest to dairy in protein (choose organic)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Use caution with rice milk:</strong> it’s high in natural sugar, low in protein, and rice products can carry more arsenic; the UK’s Food Standards Agency advises against rice drinks for young children. <strong>Skip</strong> sweetened plant milks and ones with long additive lists or carrageenan.</p>
<h3>Coffee without dairy</h3>
<p>Oat or soy milk (they froth best), a small splash of coconut cream, homemade cashew cream (1/4 cup soaked cashews blended with 1/2 cup water), a few drops of vanilla, or a dash of cinnamon.</p>
<h3>Breaking the cheese habit</h3>
<p>Cheese is the hardest to quit. Milk proteins break down into casomorphins, which have mild opioid-like effects in lab studies, and cheese’s salt-and-fat combo is hard to resist. Try nutritional yeast (naturally cheesy and nutty), minimal-ingredient cashew cheeses, and savory seasonings like garlic and onion powder. Give it 10 days: for most people, the craving fades faster than expected.</p>
<h2>What to Expect When You Quit Dairy</h2>
<p>Many people feel noticeably better within 5 to 7 days: less bloating and better digestion. Some notice their morning congestion eases (careful studies haven’t shown <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16373954/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">milk increases mucus</a>, but your journal will tell you what’s true for you). Skin often looks clearer by week 2. By week 3, energy steadies and weight loss often gets easier as hidden calories from cheese, cream, and butter disappear.</p>
<h2>Recipe: “Creamy” Cashew Sauce</h2>
<p><strong>Ingredients:</strong> 1 cup raw cashews (soaked 4 hours, or 1 hour in hot water), 1 cup filtered water, 2 tablespoons lemon juice, 1 tablespoon nutritional yeast, 1 teaspoon garlic powder, 1/2 teaspoon sea salt, and 1/4 teaspoon white pepper (optional).</p>
<p><strong>Instructions:</strong> Drain and rinse the cashews, blend everything on high for 2 to 3 minutes until silky, and adjust the seasoning. Refrigerate up to 5 days.</p>
<p>Use it as a “cream” sauce for vegetables, a salad dressing (thin with water), a sour cream swap on baked potatoes, a ranch-style dip with herbs, or a base for creamy soups. Blend in a roasted red pepper and smoked paprika for dairy-free “queso.” Tree nut allergy? Use soaked sunflower seeds.</p>

<p>You’re not losing anything you can’t get elsewhere. Calcium, protein, and creaminess all have delicious plant-based sources. What you’re leaving behind is a big source of saturated fat, sodium, and hidden calories, and maybe the bloating and skin trouble you’ve been blaming on something else.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Is dairy bad for everyone?</h3>
<p>No. Research on dairy is mixed, and some people tolerate it well. But about two-thirds of adults worldwide don’t fully digest lactose, and cheese, butter, and cream are major sources of saturated fat and sodium. A complete break is the best way to learn how your body responds.</p>
<h3>How can I get calcium without dairy?</h3>
<p>Eat calcium-rich plants daily: cooked collards, kale, bok choy, and turnip greens; broccoli; calcium-set tofu; tahini; almonds; and fortified unsweetened plant milks. Your body absorbs about 49 to 61% of the calcium in kale, bok choy, and broccoli, compared with about 32% from milk.</p>
<h3>Does milk prevent osteoporosis?</h3>
<p>Large studies following tens of thousands of adults found that drinking more milk did not protect against hip fractures. Bones need calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, vitamin K, and activity, and all can come from non-dairy sources and supplements your physician recommends.</p>
<h3>What happens to your body when you stop eating dairy?</h3>
<p>Many people notice less bloating and better digestion within 5 to 7 days, clearer skin by the second week, and easier weight loss as hidden calories from cheese, butter, and cream disappear. Results vary, so keep a simple journal.</p>
<h3>What is the healthiest milk alternative?</h3>
<p>Choose unsweetened plant milk fortified with calcium and vitamin D. Organic soy milk is closest to dairy in protein; almond, oat, coconut, and hemp are good options too. Limit rice milk, which is higher in sugar and can carry more arsenic.</p>
