<p><strong>The best low-mercury fish are the “SMASH” fish: salmon, mackerel (not king), anchovies, sardines, and herring. They’re loaded with the omega-3s your brain is built from and low in mercury because they’re small or short-lived.</strong> Eating fish at least once a week was linked to about a 60% lower risk of Alzheimer’s, and 1 to 2 servings of fatty fish a week to a 36% lower risk of dying from heart disease. Aim for 2 servings a week and skip swordfish, shark, king mackerel, and bigeye tuna.</p>
<p>Fish is the richest source of the omega-3s your brain is actually made of, and when you choose wisely, the benefits far outweigh the risks.</p>
<h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>Fatty fish is the best food source of EPA and DHA, the omega-3s in your brain cell membranes.</li>
<li>1 to 2 servings of fatty fish a week was linked to a 36% lower risk of death from heart disease.</li>
<li>Eating fish weekly was linked to about 60% lower Alzheimer’s risk.</li>
<li>Higher seafood intake raised brain mercury slightly, but that mercury wasn’t linked to Alzheimer’s brain changes.</li>
<li>SMASH fish (salmon, mackerel, anchovies, sardines, herring) give the most omega-3s with the least mercury.</li>
<li>Pregnant women and young children should choose only “best choice” fish.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Why Fish Belongs on Your Plate</h2>
<p>Your body can make only small amounts of EPA and DHA from plant omega-3s in walnuts and flax, so fatty fish is the direct route. Fish also delivers high-quality protein, vitamin D (one of the few natural food sources), vitamin B12, selenium, and iodine.</p>
<h2>One Fish, Three Battles</h2>
<h3>Fish and weight loss</h3>
<p>A 4-ounce serving of salmon has about 25 grams of protein for around 230 calories. In a <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17502874/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">European weight loss trial</a>, men who included fish in their calorie-reduced diets lost about 2 more pounds over eight weeks than men eating the same calories without fish. And every fish dinner is a dinner that isn’t fried, processed, or red meat.</p>
<h3>Fish and heart health</h3>
<p>In a <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17047219/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">landmark review in <em>JAMA</em></a>, eating 1 to 2 servings of fatty fish a week was linked to a 36% lower risk of dying from heart disease, and the researchers concluded the benefits far outweigh mercury risks for most adults. That’s why the <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29773586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">American Heart Association</a> recommends two servings of fish a week. Omega-3s lower triglycerides, steady heart rhythm, and calm inflammation.</p>
<h3>Fish and brain health</h3>
<ul>
<li>In the <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12873849/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Chicago Health and Aging Project</a>, older adults who ate fish at least once a week had about a 60% lower risk of Alzheimer’s than those who rarely or never did.</li>
<li>When <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26836731/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rush University researchers examined the brains of older adults after death</a>, those who ate more seafood had somewhat higher brain mercury, but that mercury was <em>not</em> linked to Alzheimer’s-related changes. Among people with the APOE4 gene, the strongest genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s, weekly seafood was linked to <em>less</em> Alzheimer’s pathology.</li>
<li>The <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25681666/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">MIND diet</a> recommends fish at least once a week.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Which Fish Are Lowest in Mercury?</h2>
<p>Mercury from coal burning and industry settles into water, becomes methylmercury, and builds up the food chain. Big, long-lived predators carry the most. Methylmercury is a neurotoxin, especially for a developing baby’s brain.</p>

<h3>Meet the SMASH fish</h3>
<p><strong>S</strong>almon (wild Alaskan is my favorite), <strong>M</strong>ackerel (Atlantic or Pacific, not king), <strong>A</strong>nchovies, <strong>S</strong>ardines, and <strong>H</strong>erring. They’re small or short-lived, loaded with omega-3s, low in mercury, and mostly sustainably abundant. Bonus: canned sardines with bones are a great non-dairy calcium source.</p>
<h2>Wild-Caught vs. Farmed Salmon</h2>
<p><strong>Wild Alaskan salmon</strong> is my top pick: well-managed fisheries, low contaminants, and naturally lean and flavorful.</p>
<p><strong>Farmed salmon</strong> is more complicated than its reputation:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Omega-3s:</strong> It’s fattier, so a serving often has about as many omega-3s as wild, though levels have dropped as farms moved to plant-based feed, and it has more omega-6.</li>
<li><strong>Color:</strong> Both get their pink from astaxanthin; farmed fish are fed it, often a lab-made version of the same molecule.</li>
<li><strong>Contaminants:</strong> A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14716013/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">widely publicized 2004 study</a> found higher PCBs and dioxins in farmed salmon. Levels have fallen since, but wild still tends to be lower.</li>
<li><strong>Antibiotics:</strong> Use varies enormously by country.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>My take:</strong> Choose wild-caught when you can. For farmed, look for ASC or BAP certification and check the Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch guide. Nearly all “Atlantic salmon” sold today is farmed.</p>

<h2>How Much Fish Should You Eat a Week?</h2>
<p><strong>Aim for 2 servings of low-mercury fish a week</strong>, emphasizing SMASH fish. A serving is about 4 ounces cooked, roughly the size of your palm. Try sardines on a salad Monday, wild salmon with vegetables Wednesday, and an optional mackerel, herring, or trout dinner Friday.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Safety check:</strong> <strong>Pregnant, breastfeeding, or feeding young children:</strong> fish supports a baby’s developing brain, but choose only “best choices,” aim for 8 to 12 ounces a week, avoid high-mercury fish, and skip raw or undercooked fish. <strong>Blood thinners:</strong> eating fish is fine, but high-dose fish oil supplements can add to bleeding risk, so ask your physician. <strong>Fish allergy:</strong> get omega-3s from walnuts, flax, chia, and hemp, and ask your physician about algae-based DHA. <strong>Salt:</strong> choose low-sodium canned fish packed in water or olive oil. <strong>Doneness:</strong> cook fish to 145°F or until it flakes easily.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Do Mercury Detoxes Work?</h2>
<p>Cilantro, chlorella, and “heavy metal detox” products have weak human evidence, and some carry their own risks. Your body naturally clears methylmercury from food over weeks to months. The best “detox” is choosing low-mercury fish. What genuinely helps:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Selenium:</strong> many ocean fish are rich in it, and it binds mercury. One or two Brazil nuts a few times a week also provide plenty (no more; too much selenium is harmful).</li>
<li><strong>A fiber-rich, plant-forward diet.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Variety</strong> in your fish choices.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you’re worried about mercury exposure, ask your physician about a blood test. Chelation should only be done under medical supervision for diagnosed poisoning.</p>
<h2>Mold-Risk Foods: A Quick Recap</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Peanuts and peanut butter:</strong> the most common aflatoxin source. Swap in almond butter, sunflower seed butter, or tahini.</li>
<li><strong>Corn products:</strong> can carry aflatoxin and fumonisins. Buy fresh from reputable brands.</li>
<li><strong>Coffee:</strong> ochratoxin A is usually well below limits, and roasting reduces it. Choose fresh, well-stored beans.</li>
<li><strong>Dried fruit:</strong> can carry ochratoxin A. Choose fresh fruit day to day.</li>
<li><strong>Wine and beer:</strong> the bigger brain issue is the alcohol. The <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39096926/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2024 <em>Lancet</em> Commission</a> lists heavy drinking as a modifiable dementia risk factor, and newer research suggests no amount of alcohol is good for the brain.</li>
</ul>
<p>Buy fresh, store dry and sealed, inspect before eating, and when in doubt, throw it out. Read more in our <a href="/blog/healthy-breakfast-weight-loss-processed-breakfast-foods">healthy breakfast guide</a>.</p>
<h2>Recipe: SMASH Fish Mediterranean Bowl</h2>
<p><strong>Bowl:</strong> 6 oz wild Alaskan salmon (or 1 can wild sardines), 2 cups mixed greens, 1 cup halved cherry tomatoes, 1 diced cucumber, 1/4 thinly sliced red onion, 1/2 sliced avocado, 2 tablespoons capers, 1/4 cup olives, 2 tablespoons hemp seeds, and fresh dill and parsley.</p>
<p><strong>Lemon dressing:</strong> 3 tablespoons lemon juice, 2 tablespoons extra virgin olive oil, 1 minced garlic clove, 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard, 1/2 teaspoon dried oregano, and salt and pepper.</p>
<p><strong>Instructions:</strong></p>
<ol type="1">
<li>Season the salmon with salt, pepper, and dill, and cook in a little olive oil over medium heat about 4 to 5 minutes per side, until it flakes (145°F). Flake into pieces. (Or drain and break up the sardines; the soft bones are full of calcium.)</li>
<li>Whisk the dressing.</li>
<li>Layer the greens, vegetables, avocado, fish, capers, olives, and hemp seeds. Drizzle and garnish with herbs.</li>
</ol>
<p>Capers and olives are salty, so go easy on added salt. <strong>5-minute lunch:</strong> mash sardines with lemon, Dijon, and herbs, and spread on whole grain sourdough.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What fish is lowest in mercury?</h3>
<p>Salmon, sardines, anchovies, Atlantic mackerel, herring, trout, cod, pollock, shrimp, scallops, and canned light tuna are among the FDA’s “best choices.” The SMASH fish (salmon, mackerel, anchovies, sardines, herring) combine low mercury with high omega-3s.</p>
<h3>What fish should you avoid because of mercury?</h3>
<p>The FDA and EPA advise avoiding king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, Gulf of Mexico tilefish, and bigeye tuna. These large, long-lived predators build up the most mercury.</p>
<h3>Is fish good for your brain?</h3>
<p>Yes. Eating fish at least once a week was linked to about a 60% lower risk of Alzheimer’s, and brain studies found seafood’s small mercury increase wasn’t linked to Alzheimer’s changes. Fatty fish supplies DHA, a major structural fat in the brain.</p>
<h3>Is farmed salmon healthy?</h3>
<p>Farmed salmon can be a reasonable choice. It often has similar omega-3s to wild salmon but more omega-6, and historically higher contaminant levels. Choose wild when you can, and for farmed, look for ASC or BAP certification.</p>
<h3>How much fish should I eat per week?</h3>
<p>The American Heart Association recommends two servings a week, especially fatty fish. A serving is about 4 ounces cooked. Pregnant women should aim for 8 to 12 ounces a week of “best choice” fish.</p>
