If the goal of 10,000 steps a day has ever made you close the app and give up, here is some encouraging news. You may not need that number for your brain.

In 2025, researchers in the Harvard Aging Brain Study reported a careful look at walking, brain changes, and thinking skills. They followed cognitively healthy older adults, some for as long as 14 years, and measured daily steps with a pedometer rather than relying on memory. In people who already had elevated amyloid, a protein that can build up in the brain in Alzheimer's disease, higher step counts were associated with slower buildup of tau, another protein tied to Alzheimer's, and with slower cognitive decline. That link leveled off around 5,000 to 7,500 steps a day.

This was an observational study. It shows an association, not proof that walking will change the course of Alzheimer's for any one person. Even so, it offers a reachable goal, especially if you have been told that only a big exercise plan "counts."

## Key Takeaways

- In the Harvard Aging Brain Study, higher daily steps were associated with slower tau buildup and slower decline in thinking and daily function, but mainly in people who already had elevated amyloid.
- The association appeared to level off at a moderate level of activity, about 5,001 to 7,500 steps a day.
- Even a lower range, about 3,001 to 5,000 steps a day, was associated with slower changes than being largely inactive (about 3,000 steps or fewer).
- Walking was not associated with lower amyloid itself. The link was with slower tau buildup and slower decline in people who already had elevated amyloid.
- The study cannot prove cause and effect. It is still a hopeful, practical finding for people who want a goal they can actually meet.

## What the Researchers Measured

The paper, published in Nature Medicine, included 296 adults who were cognitively healthy at the start. They were ages 50 to 90. Each person wore a pedometer for about a week at baseline so researchers could estimate average steps per day. Many also had brain scans that measure amyloid and tau, and they took thinking tests year after year, for a median of a little more than nine years, and up to 14 years.

Amyloid and tau are proteins. In Alzheimer's disease, amyloid can collect in plaques, and tau can form tangles that are closely tied to symptoms. You do not need to memorize those terms. The plain-language point is this: the researchers were not only asking "Who stayed sharp?" They were also watching early biological changes, years before dementia would be diagnosed.

Among people with elevated amyloid at the start, those who walked more had a slower rise in tau in a brain region involved early in Alzheimer's, and a slower slide in thinking and everyday function. In people with low amyloid, there was little decline over time, and walking was not clearly linked with extra benefit in that group.

## Why 5,000 to 7,500 Steps Is the Number to Remember

The team sorted activity into ranges: inactive (3,000 steps or fewer a day), low (3,001 to 5,000), moderate (5,001 to 7,500), and active (7,501 or more). Compared with inactive people who had elevated amyloid, even the low-activity group had slower tau buildup and slower cognitive decline. Moderate activity was associated with further slowing. After that, the curve flattened. People who took 7,501 or more steps did not show a clear extra gain over the moderate group.

That is why "you don't need 10,000 steps" is a fair headline, as long as we stay honest about what the study can and cannot say. Ten thousand steps may still be a fine goal for heart health or general fitness. For this brain-health association, the benefit appeared to plateau in the 5,000 to 7,500 range.

A Harvard Gazette summary of the same paper described model estimates in which reaching a threshold of cognitive worsening took longer in the more active groups than in the inactive group. Those are estimates from the data, not a stopwatch on Alzheimer's. The safer way to say it is the way the scientists did: more steps were associated with slower decline, up to a moderate daily total.

## A Goal You Can Work Toward

If you are already near 5,000 steps, you may not need a new athletic identity. If you are well below that, you do not have to get there tomorrow. Adding a short walk after a meal, parking a little farther away, or taking a phone call on foot can raise a daily count over time. Every step counts, and small increases can add up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many steps a day are linked with brain health in this study?

The association with slower tau buildup and slower cognitive decline leveled off around 5,000 to 7,500 steps a day in people with elevated amyloid. Even about 3,000 to 5,000 steps was associated with slower changes than being largely inactive.

### Does walking prevent Alzheimer's disease?

This study cannot say that. It was observational, which means researchers watched what people were already doing. Higher step counts were associated with slower changes in some people. That is not the same as proof that walking prevents or delays Alzheimer's for an individual.

### What are amyloid and tau?

They are proteins involved in Alzheimer's disease. Amyloid can build up in the brain years before symptoms. Tau buildup is more closely tied to the decline in thinking. In this study, walking was linked with slower tau buildup, not with less amyloid.

### Do I need a pedometer or a smartwatch?

The study used a pedometer, but you do not need a particular device to start walking more. A phone, a watch, or a simple daily route you can count is enough to get going. If you use a wearable, treat the number as a guide, not a grade.
