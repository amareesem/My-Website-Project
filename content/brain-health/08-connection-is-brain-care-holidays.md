The day after Thanksgiving can be full, or it can feel oddly quiet. The dishes are done. Some guests have gone home. It is a natural moment to ask a simple question: who are you close to, and when did you last reach out?

That question is not small talk. Research from the National Institute on Aging associates social isolation and loneliness with a higher risk of cognitive decline, Alzheimer's disease, depression, and heart disease. Social isolation is also one of the Lancet Commission's list of dementia risk factors that people and communities can work on. Of the known risk factors for dementia, more than 80% are modifiable, and the Lancet Commission estimates that addressing just 14 of them could prevent or delay up to 45% of dementia cases worldwide.

Those figures describe populations, not a personal score. They also do not mean that a quiet season will harm your brain, or that you have failed if the holidays feel complicated. Connection is brain care. It is also ordinary human care. We can talk about it with hope, and without guilt.

## Key Takeaways

- Social isolation and loneliness are associated with higher risk of cognitive decline, Alzheimer's disease, depression, and heart disease, according to the National Institute on Aging.
- Isolation is one of the 14 dementia risk factors on the Lancet Commission's list of things we can work on.
- Isolation and loneliness are related, but they are not the same. You can be alone and content, or surrounded by people and still feel lonely.

## Isolation, Loneliness, and the Brain

Social isolation means having few relationships or infrequent contact with other people. Loneliness is the unhappy feeling that can come when the connection you have does not match the connection you want. A person can live alone and feel fine. A person can sit at a crowded table and feel unseen. Both isolation and loneliness have been linked, in research, with harms that go beyond mood, including thinking skills and heart health.

Scientists are still working out the "why." Possible paths include stress, sleep, movement, and the simple fact that conversation and shared activity give the brain something to do. We do not need a perfect mechanism to take the finding seriously. We only need to treat other people as part of a brain-health plan, the same way we treat walking or sleep.

## Leading With Connection, Not With Fear

The holidays can put a spotlight on who is missing. They can also be hard for caregivers, who may be tired, or for people whose family relationships are strained. None of this is a test you can fail.

If you are caring for someone, or missing someone, the invitation is small. Call the person you have been meaning to call. Accept one invitation, or extend one. If a big gathering is too much, a walk, a shared leftover lunch, or a weekly video call can still count. The point is regular contact, not a perfect holiday card.

NIA's tips for staying connected are practical and kind. They include:

- Volunteering, which gives you a role and a reason to show up
- Taking a class, or joining a book club, choir, walking group, or place of worship
- Scheduling regular check-ins with a family member, friend, or neighbor
- Finding group activities that match your energy and mobility, including options in your community or online when leaving home is hard

You do not have to do all of these. Pick one that feels like a fit, not a chore.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are loneliness and social isolation the same thing?

No. Isolation is about how much contact you have. Loneliness is how you feel about that contact. Both have been associated with health risks, including risks to thinking and memory.

### Does staying social prevent dementia?

No one can promise that. Social isolation is one of the risk factors on the Lancet Commission's list, and research associates isolation and loneliness with higher risk of cognitive decline. Staying connected is a wise form of care, not a guarantee.

### What if the holidays make connection feel harder, not easier?

That is common, especially for caregivers or for people who are grieving. A smaller plan can still help: one call, one walk, one regular check-in. There is no prize for a packed calendar.
