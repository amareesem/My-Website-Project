Research reviewed through September 2026

Where the science stands today

Nearly half of dementia is linked to things we can change

The Lancet Commission, the world's leading panel of dementia experts, estimates that about 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors.

Combined lifestyle programs protect the brain

Large randomized trials in Finland, the United States, and 11 Latin American countries show that a structured program combining nutrition, exercise, brain training, social connection, and health monitoring protects thinking and memory in people at risk.

Risk can now be seen years earlier

Blood biomarkers, genetic testing, and cognitive testing now let physicians identify risk long before symptoms appear, while there is still time to act.

The gut is part of the brain's story

Changes in gut bacteria appear years before memory symptoms, and in 2026 researchers pinpointed a gut-bacteria compound linked to Alzheimer's brain changes.

How the science climbed

For most of the last century, dementia was treated as bad luck and old age. Here is how that picture changed, one discovery at a time.

  1. 1906

    A disease gets a name

    Dr. Alois Alzheimer describes the plaques and tangles he found in the brain of a woman who died with severe memory loss. For decades, those deposits were seen as the whole story.

  2. 1986–2001

    The Nun Study reveals "cognitive reserve"

    Researchers followed 678 Catholic sisters through old age and studied their brains after death. Some sisters whose brains were full of plaques and tangles had shown no signs of dementia in life. The lesson: the life you live helps decide whether brain changes ever become symptoms.

  3. 1992–1993

    Amyloid takes center stage, and APOE4 is found

    The "amyloid hypothesis" becomes the dominant theory and drives most drug research for the next 30 years. A year later, scientists identify APOE4 as the strongest common gene linked to Alzheimer's. It raises risk, but it does not guarantee disease.

  4. 2011–2013

    The brain proves it can change

    A year of brisk walking enlarges the hippocampus, the brain's memory center, in older adults. Scientists discover the glymphatic system, the brain's overnight cleaning crew that clears waste, including amyloid, mostly during sleep. Genetic studies put the brain's immune cells, the microglia, at the center of Alzheimer's risk.

  5. 2014

    Root-cause medicine enters the conversation

    Dr. Dale Bredesen reports improved cognition in a small group of patients treated with a personalized, multi-part protocol. It was small and uncontrolled, but it opened a new question: what if each person's decline has its own set of drivers?

  6. 2015

    FINGER: the first major proof that combination works

    In Finland, 1,260 older adults at risk of decline were randomly assigned to a two-year program of nutrition, exercise, brain training, and heart-health monitoring, or to general health advice. The combined program improved overall cognition 25% more than the comparison group, with even larger gains in executive function and processing speed.

  7. 2017–2020

    The experts put a number on prevention

    The Lancet Commission estimates that 35% of dementia is linked to nine modifiable risk factors (2017), then 40% across twelve (2020). In 2019, the SPRINT MIND trial shows that tighter blood pressure control lowers the risk of mild cognitive impairment.

  8. 2023

    Conventional medicine changes the biology

    The first anti-amyloid drugs gain FDA approval after slowing decline in early Alzheimer's by roughly a quarter to a third over 18 months. The benefit is modest and the risks are real, but it proves the disease process can be altered. The same year, the ACHIEVE trial shows that treating hearing loss slows cognitive decline in older adults at higher risk.

  9. 2023

    The gut–brain link comes into focus

    Researchers at Washington University find that people with the earliest brain signs of Alzheimer's, but no symptoms, already carry different gut bacteria. In Ireland, rats given gut bacteria from people with Alzheimer's develop memory problems and grow fewer new brain cells.

  10. 2024

    45%, and lifestyle helps people already diagnosed

    The Lancet Commission adds high LDL cholesterol and untreated vision loss, bringing the list to 14 risk factors and the estimate to about 45%. Dr. Dean Ornish's randomized trial shows that intensive lifestyle changes improved cognition and function measures over 20 weeks in people with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's.

  11. 2025

    U.S. POINTER, and surprising new clues

    The largest U.S. lifestyle trial, with 2,111 at-risk adults, confirms FINGER and shows that structure and coaching amplify the benefit. A natural experiment in Wales links the shingles vaccine to 20% fewer dementia diagnoses. Harvard scientists report that lithium, a trace element, is depleted early in Alzheimer's brains. The FDA clears the first blood tests to aid Alzheimer's diagnosis.

  12. 2026

    The newest evidence

    A 20-year follow-up finds that a few weeks of brain-speed training, with booster sessions, was linked to 25% fewer dementia diagnoses two decades later. LatAm-FINGERS shows the structured lifestyle approach works across 11 countries and cultures. A large study in JAMA finds a p-tau217 blood test can signal risk up to 10 years before symptoms, and in August the FDA clears a p-tau217 blood test for use in primary care. University of Wisconsin scientists identify a compound made by gut bacteria that is linked to Alzheimer's brain changes and faster decline.

Each of these milestones is described in more detail below, with its source.

Seven fields, pointing the same way

Conventional medicine and root-cause medicine don't always agree. On this question, they increasingly do. Here is what each field has contributed, and where you'll find it inside PEAK.

Randomized controlled trial: the strongest test of cause and effect Large population study: strong patterns in real-world data Early, small, or laboratory research: promising, still being confirmed

Neuroscience

The brain can build, repair, and clean itself, when we give it what it needs.

For generations, doctors were taught that adult brains could only decline. Neuroscience overturned that. The brain grows new connections throughout life, it has a waste-clearing system that runs mostly while we sleep, and its own immune cells can either protect it or inflame it, depending on the signals they receive.

Exercise grows the memory center

In a randomized trial of 120 older adults, a year of aerobic walking increased the size of the hippocampus by about 2%, roughly reversing one to two years of age-related shrinkage, and improved memory.

Erickson et al., PNAS, 2011. Randomized controlled trial

Sleep is when the brain takes out the trash

Researchers discovered that during sleep, the spaces between brain cells widen and cerebrospinal fluid flushes out waste products, including amyloid. This was first shown in animal studies.

Xie et al., Science, 2013. Laboratory research

Short sleep in midlife raises later risk

In a 25-year study of about 8,000 British adults, people who regularly slept six hours or less in their 50s and 60s had roughly a 30% higher risk of developing dementia.

Sabia et al., Nature Communications, 2021. Large population study

Lifestyle helps even when the brain already shows changes

Brain imaging from the U.S. POINTER trial found that people with smaller hippocampal volume or more tau buildup gained more cognitive benefit from the structured lifestyle program than similar people in the self-guided group. Amyloid buildup did not reduce the benefit.

U.S. POINTER neuroimaging ancillary study, presented at CTAD, December 2025. Randomized trial substudy

Genetics

Genes load the dice. They don't decide the roll.

Fewer than 1% of Alzheimer's cases are caused by genes that make the disease a near certainty. For everyone else, genes shift the odds. The most important of these, APOE4, is carried by about one in four people. Knowing your genetic risk doesn't seal your fate. It tells you how early, and how seriously, to act.

APOE4: the strongest common risk gene

Identified in 1993, one copy of APOE4 raises Alzheimer's risk several-fold, and two copies raise it much further. A 2024 study concluded that carrying two copies represents a distinct genetic form of Alzheimer's, which makes early prevention especially important for these families.

Corder et al., Science, 1993; Fortea et al., Nature Medicine, 2024

A healthy lifestyle helps even with high genetic risk

Among nearly 200,000 older adults in the UK Biobank, people at high genetic risk who followed a favorable lifestyle had about a 32% lower risk of dementia than those at high genetic risk with an unfavorable lifestyle.

Lourida et al., JAMA, 2019. Large population study

APOE4 carriers benefit from lifestyle programs too

In both FINGER and U.S. POINTER, the cognitive benefits of the lifestyle program were seen in people who carry APOE4, not only in those who don't.

Solomon et al., JAMA Neurology, 2018; Baker et al., JAMA, 2025

Lifestyle can change how genes are expressed

In a small eight-week pilot trial, a diet and lifestyle program lowered participants' epigenetic age, a DNA-based measure of biological aging, by about three years compared with controls.

Fitzgerald et al., Aging, 2021. Small pilot randomized trial

Conventional medicine

The medical mainstream now agrees: dementia risk can be lowered.

The most conservative voices in medicine have moved. Major medical bodies now publish risk-reduction guidance, the FDA is clearing blood tests for early detection, and the largest research organizations are funding prevention trials. The 14 risk factors identified by the Lancet Commission are less education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, excessive alcohol, social isolation, air pollution, and untreated vision loss.

The Lancet Commission: about 45% potentially preventable

The 2024 report estimates that addressing all 14 risk factors could prevent or delay about 45% of dementia worldwide. Hearing loss and high LDL cholesterol in midlife each account for about 7%.

Livingston et al., The Lancet, 2024. Expert synthesis of population evidence

Blood pressure control lowers risk of cognitive impairment

In more than 9,000 adults, aiming for a systolic blood pressure under 120 lowered the risk of mild cognitive impairment by 19% compared with standard targets.

SPRINT MIND Investigators, JAMA, 2019. Randomized controlled trial

Treating hearing loss protects thinking

Among older adults with hearing loss who were at higher risk of decline, hearing care slowed cognitive decline by 48% over three years. No significant effect was seen in the lower-risk group.

Lin et al. (ACHIEVE), The Lancet, 2023. Randomized controlled trial

The shingles vaccine and fewer dementia diagnoses

A strict birthday cutoff for shingles vaccination in Wales created a natural experiment among about 280,000 older adults. Those who received the vaccine were 20% less likely to be diagnosed with dementia over seven years, and the researchers have found similar signals in other countries.

Eyting et al., Nature, 2025. Natural experiment

Drugs can change the disease, but modestly

Anti-amyloid drugs slowed decline in early Alzheimer's by roughly 27% to 35% over 18 months, with a risk of brain swelling and bleeding. In late 2025, two large trials of the diabetes drug semaglutide in early Alzheimer's did not slow the disease. Now the Alzheimer's Association has launched a $100 million trial, PROTECT-Cog, to test whether a metabolic drug adds benefit when paired with the U.S. POINTER lifestyle program. Lifestyle has become the foundation that new treatments are built on.

van Dyck et al., NEJM, 2023; Sims et al., JAMA, 2023; Alzheimer's Association, AAIC 2026

Blood tests can reveal risk years before symptoms

In a study of nearly 2,700 cognitively healthy older adults, those with very high p-tau217 levels had about a 78% chance of developing cognitive impairment within 10 years. In a separate real-world study in Sweden, family doctors diagnosed Alzheimer's correctly 93% of the time with a blood test result in hand, up from 65% without it. In August 2026, the FDA cleared the Elecsys p-tau217 blood test for use in primary and specialty care.

JAMA, 2026 and AAIC 2026; Roche and Alzheimer's Association announcements, August 24, 2026

A note on guidelines: current Alzheimer's Association guidance reserves these blood tests for people who already have memory or thinking symptoms, and researchers say screening healthy people is not yet routine practice. Lifestyle medicine

The strongest medicine we have is a combination, not a single change.

This is where the evidence is deepest. Each lifestyle habit on its own shows benefit in population studies. But when researchers test them in randomized trials, the clearest results come from programs that combine several changes at once and give people structure and support to keep them.

U.S. POINTER: structure and support make the difference

2,111 adults aged 60 to 79 at risk of decline, from diverse communities across five U.S. sites, followed a two-year program of exercise, the MIND diet, BrainHQ brain training, social and intellectual challenge, and regular health check-ins. Both groups improved, but the structured, coached group improved more, an effect estimated at one to two years of slower cognitive aging. The structured group also saw better blood pressure regulation and fewer sleep apnea events.

Baker et al., JAMA, 2025; ancillary studies presented at CTAD, December 2025. Randomized controlled trial

LatAm-FINGERS: it works across cultures

More than 1,000 older adults in 11 Latin American countries followed a culturally adapted version of the same approach, with salsa and tango for exercise and local foods for nutrition. Those who received structured coaching and peer support showed 55% greater improvement in overall memory, thinking, and reasoning than those following a self-guided program over two years.

The Lancet, 2026, presented at AAIC 2026. Randomized controlled trial

Brain-speed training: 20 years of follow-up

In the ACTIVE trial of 2,802 adults aged 65 and older, people who completed a few weeks of adaptive processing-speed training plus booster sessions had 25% fewer dementia diagnoses over 20 years. Memory and reasoning training did not show the same long-term effect.

Coe et al., Alzheimer's & Dementia: TRCI, 2026. Randomized controlled trial

Every step counts

Among 78,000 UK adults, walking about 9,800 steps a day was linked to about a 50% lower risk of dementia, and even about 3,800 steps a day was linked to about a 25% lower risk.

del Pozo Cruz et al., JAMA Neurology, 2022. Large population study

One change alone isn't enough

A three-year trial of the MIND diet found that participants who followed it did no better than a comparison group on a different healthy, calorie-reduced diet. Both groups improved. It's an important lesson: diet matters, but it works best as one part of a larger plan.

Barnes et al., NEJM, 2023. Randomized controlled trial

Functional & precision medicine

Find the drivers of decline, person by person, and address them.

Root-cause medicine starts from a simple observation: cognitive decline has many possible contributors, including insulin resistance, inflammation, nutrient gaps, hormone changes, toxins, infections, and poor sleep, and the mix is different for every person. The goal is to find your drivers and treat them. This is the youngest body of evidence on the page, and it is growing fast.

The first randomized trial of a precision protocol

In a nine-month trial, 73 people with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia were randomly assigned to a personalized precision-medicine protocol or standard care. The precision group showed greater improvements in memory, executive function, and processing speed, along with better blood pressure, blood sugar, and lipids. Reviewers have noted that Alzheimer's blood biomarkers and brain volume did not change measurably over the nine months, and the results had not completed peer review as of this writing.

Bredesen and colleagues, preprint, December 2025. Randomized trial awaiting peer review

Earlier pilot studies

In a 2022 pilot trial, 25 people with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's followed a personalized protocol for nine months and showed statistically significant improvements on cognitive testing. A 2023 study from Dr. Heather Sandison's residential program reported similar improvements.

Toups et al., Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, 2022; Sandison et al., Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, 2023. Small uncontrolled studies

B vitamins slowed brain shrinkage

In older adults with mild cognitive impairment, two years of B vitamins slowed the rate of brain shrinkage, with the greatest benefit in people who had high homocysteine. Later analysis suggested the benefit depended on having good omega-3 levels, a reminder that nutrients work together.

Smith et al., PLoS One, 2010; Douaud et al., PNAS, 2013; Oulhaj et al., J Alzheimer's Dis, 2016. Randomized controlled trial

A ketogenic diet improved daily function

In a small 12-week crossover trial of people with Alzheimer's, a modified ketogenic diet improved daily function and quality of life compared with a usual diet.

Phillips et al., Alzheimer's Research & Therapy, 2021. Small randomized trial

Metabolic health is brain health

Diabetes, obesity, and high LDL cholesterol are all on the Lancet Commission's list of risk factors. Insulin resistance in the brain is so closely tied to Alzheimer's that some researchers have called it "type 3 diabetes."

Livingston et al., The Lancet, 2024; de la Monte & Wands, 2005

Integrative & mind-body medicine

Low-risk practices with growing scientific support.

Practices once dismissed as "alternative," like meditation, tai chi, and sauna, are now being studied seriously. The trials are smaller than those in lifestyle medicine, so we're careful here. We include practices that are safe, that line up with stronger evidence about stress, circulation, and inflammation, and that people actually enjoy doing.

Intensive lifestyle change, including stress management

In Dr. Dean Ornish's trial of 51 people with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's, a program of whole-food plant-based nutrition, exercise, meditation, yoga, and group support improved several measures of cognition and function over 20 weeks, while the control group worsened. The blood amyloid ratio improved in the lifestyle group, and the gut microbiome changed. In a 40-week extension, the gains continued.

Ornish et al., Alzheimer's Research & Therapy, 2024; 40-week results, Alzheimer's & Dementia, 2025. Small randomized controlled trial

Tai chi

In a 2023 randomized trial in older adults with mild cognitive impairment, tai chi, especially a version that added thinking challenges, improved global cognition scores more than stretching over six months.

Li et al., JAMA Network Open, 2023. Randomized controlled trial

Meditation

In a small 12-week trial of adults with early memory concerns, a daily 12-minute meditation practice improved memory and cognitive performance, and the gains held at six months.

Innes et al., Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, 2017. Small randomized trial

Sauna

In a 20-year study of more than 2,000 Finnish men, those who used a sauna four to seven times a week had about a 66% lower risk of dementia than those who used it once a week.

Laukkanen et al., Age and Ageing, 2017. Population study

Longevity medicine

Slow the aging that drives brain disease.

Age is the single biggest risk factor for dementia. Longevity science asks why cells age: chronic inflammation, failing mitochondria, and a slowdown in autophagy, the process cells use to clear out damaged parts. Much of this research is still in the lab, but it explains why the lifestyle trials work, and it points to what's next.

A daily multivitamin modestly slowed cognitive aging

Across three COSMOS trials with more than 5,000 older adults, a daily multivitamin improved cognition by the equivalent of about two years of slower cognitive aging.

Vyas et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 2024. Randomized controlled trials

Lithium: a newly discovered vulnerability

Harvard researchers found that lithium, a trace element naturally present in the brain, is depleted early in Alzheimer's as amyloid plaques bind it up. In mice, restoring normal lithium levels with lithium orotate reversed brain changes and memory loss. Human trials are needed, and lithium should never be self-prescribed.

Aron et al., Nature, 2025. Laboratory research

Biological age can move in both directions

Epigenetic clocks measure how fast your body is aging at the cellular level. Early trials suggest that diet and lifestyle can slow these clocks, which gives us a way to track progress long before memory changes would show up.

Fitzgerald et al., Aging, 2021. Small pilot trial

The gut–brain connection

Trillions of microbes in your gut are in constant conversation with your brain. The newest research suggests that conversation helps shape your risk of dementia.

Your gut is home to trillions of bacteria and other microbes, together called the microbiome. Twenty years ago, few brain researchers paid it much attention. Today it is one of the fastest-moving areas in dementia science, with major discoveries arriving every year. Scientists have mapped four main ways the gut and brain stay in touch.

The vagus nerve

A direct nerve line runs from the gut to the brain, carrying signals in both directions.

The immune system

When the gut lining becomes "leaky," bacterial fragments such as LPS can slip into the bloodstream and fuel inflammation throughout the body, including in the brain, where it can activate microglia.

Microbial compounds

Gut bacteria make substances that travel in the blood. Some, like butyrate made from dietary fiber, help protect the gut barrier and calm inflammation. Others appear to harm the brain.

Metabolism

Gut microbes influence blood sugar, cholesterol, blood pressure, and weight, several of which are on the Lancet Commission's list of dementia risk factors.

What the gut tells us about the brain

Evidence linking the microbiome to Alzheimer's

People with Alzheimer's carry different gut bacteria

A University of Wisconsin study was among the first to show that the gut microbiome of people with Alzheimer's differs clearly from that of people without it, and that some of those differences track with Alzheimer's markers in spinal fluid.

Vogt et al., Scientific Reports, 2017. Observational study

The changes appear before symptoms

Among 164 cognitively normal older adults, the 49 who already showed amyloid and tau changes on brain scans and spinal fluid tests had distinctly different gut bacteria, even though their diets were similar. The differences tracked with amyloid and tau, not with brain cell loss, suggesting the gut changes early. A 2026 Australian study reported similar findings.

Ferreiro et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2023; Dissanayaka et al., Nutrients, 2026. Observational studies

Gut bacteria can transfer memory problems

When researchers transplanted gut bacteria from people with Alzheimer's into young rats, the rats developed memory problems and made fewer new cells in the hippocampus. The more impaired the human donor, the more impaired the rat.

Grabrucker et al., Brain, 2023. Laboratory research

Bacterial fragments are found in Alzheimer's brains

LPS, a fragment of gut-type bacteria that triggers inflammation, was found in Alzheimer's brain tissue at higher levels than in healthy brains, clustered around amyloid plaques.

Zhan et al., Neurology, 2016. Laboratory research

New in 2026: a gut compound linked to Alzheimer's

In 1,196 cognitively healthy adults, higher blood levels of imidazole propionate, a compound made by certain gut bacteria, were linked to lower cognitive scores, more Alzheimer's biomarkers, and faster decline over time. A gene variant that raises levels of the compound was also linked to Alzheimer's risk, which points toward cause rather than coincidence. In mice, the compound weakened the blood-brain barrier and worsened amyloid and tau buildup.

Vemuganti et al., Nature Communications, 2026. Population study with laboratory research

What changes the gut, and helps the brain

Evidence that improving the microbiome can help

Diet reshapes the aging gut

In a one-year trial of 612 older adults across five European countries, a Mediterranean-style diet increased gut bacteria linked to less inflammation, less frailty, and better cognitive function, and reduced bacteria linked to poorer health.

Ghosh et al. (NU-AGE), Gut, 2020. Randomized controlled trial

Lifestyle change shifted the microbiome, alongside cognitive gains

In Dr. Dean Ornish's trial in people with mild cognitive impairment or early Alzheimer's, the gut microbiome improved in the lifestyle group but not in the control group, alongside improvements in cognition and function.

Ornish et al., Alzheimer's Research & Therapy, 2024. Small randomized controlled trial

A daily fiber supplement sharpened memory

In a double-blind trial of 36 pairs of twins aged 60 and older, the twin who took a daily prebiotic fiber (inulin and FOS) for 12 weeks grew more beneficial Bifidobacterium and made fewer errors on a memory test used to detect early Alzheimer's than the twin who took a placebo. A longer follow-up trial is underway.

Ni Lochlainn et al., Nature Communications, 2024. Randomized controlled trial

Probiotics show modest benefit

A 2025 analysis combining 15 randomized trials and nearly 1,000 people with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's found that probiotics improved cognitive scores compared with placebo. The trials were small and used many different strains, and results are less consistent once dementia is established.

Peng et al., meta-analysis of randomized trials, 2025

The mouth matters too

Toxins from Porphyromonas gingivalis, a bacterium behind gum disease, have been found in Alzheimer's brains. A drug aimed at those toxins did not meet its main goals in a clinical trial, so the story is not simple, but oral health is increasingly seen as part of brain health.

Dominy et al., Science Advances, 2019. Laboratory research

What we still don't know: most of this evidence shows strong links rather than final proof, and not every study agrees. A 2026 Finnish study that followed about 4,000 people for 16 years found only a modest overall link between the microbiome and later dementia, pointing to specific bacteria rather than overall diversity. Scientists are still working out which microbes and compounds matter most, and results from the U.S. POINTER microbiome study are still to come. What is consistent is this: the fiber-rich, plant-forward eating patterns that feed a healthy microbiome are the same ones used in the dementia-prevention trials. Fecal transplants and high-dose probiotics are not proven ways to prevent dementia.

Reading the evidence with clear eyes

Lower risk, not a guarantee

The science shows that risk can be reduced. No program, including ours, can promise that any one person will never develop dementia.

What "45%" really means

The Lancet estimate describes whole populations. It means that if all 14 risk factors disappeared, about 45% of dementia cases might be prevented or delayed. It doesn't mean your own risk falls by exactly that amount.

Not all evidence is equal

A randomized trial, a population study, and a mouse study each tell us something different. That's why every finding on this page is marked, so you can see how solid it is.

The science keeps climbing

Some of the most exciting findings, like lithium and the newest precision-medicine trial, are still early. We share them because they matter, and we label them because you deserve to know the difference.
