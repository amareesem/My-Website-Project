Ancient Grains: Quinoa, Farro, Barley, and More for Weight Loss and Brain Health

Ancient grains like quinoa, farro, and barley bring more protein, fiber, and minerals than white rice. Benefits, gluten guide, and how to cook them.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 7 min read

Ancient grains like quinoa, farro, barley, amaranth, millet, and buckwheat are whole grains that have changed little over centuries and are usually eaten intact rather than ground into white flour. That means more protein, fiber, and minerals. A cup of cooked quinoa has about twice the protein and several times the fiber of white rice, and barley’s beta-glucan lowers LDL cholesterol by about 7%. They make it easy to hit the MIND diet’s goal of three whole grain servings a day.

These aren’t trendy health foods. They’re some of the original staple foods that sustained entire civilizations, and they’re about to make your whole grain upgrade a lot more interesting than brown rice three times a day.

Key Takeaways

  • Ancient grains are usually eaten as intact whole grains, so you get all their fiber, minerals, and plant compounds.
  • Quinoa, amaranth, and buckwheat are complete proteins, rare in the plant world.
  • About 3 grams of barley beta-glucan a day lowered LDL cholesterol by about 7%.
  • People who eat 30 or more different plant foods a week have more diverse gut microbiomes.
  • Farro, barley, spelt, einkorn, and Kamut contain gluten; quinoa, amaranth, millet, and buckwheat don’t.

Ancient Grains vs. Modern Wheat

Modern wheat has been bred over the last century, through traditional crossbreeding rather than genetic engineering, for shorter, sturdier stalks and much bigger harvests. That’s how the world avoided mass famine in the 1960s and ’70s.

You’ll often hear that modern wheat was bred to contain far more gluten. But when a USDA scientist reviewed a century of data, he found no clear evidence that the gluten content of U.S. wheat has risen. The bigger problem isn’t the wheat plant. It’s grinding it into white flour and turning it into ultra-processed food.

Ancient grains have changed relatively little and are usually eaten whole, so nothing gets stripped away.

Why organic? Some conventional grain crops are sprayed with glyphosate shortly before harvest to dry them out, which can leave residues. Scientists still disagree about what that means for people, but organic farming doesn’t permit glyphosate, so choosing organic sidesteps the debate.

One Ancient Grain, Three Battles

Ancient grains and weight loss

In a 6-week controlled trial, swapping refined grains for whole grains worked out to about 100 calories a day in people’s favor. Ancient grains bring that advantage plus extra protein. A cup of cooked quinoa has about 8 grams of protein and 5 grams of fiber, roughly twice the protein and several times the fiber of white rice (USDA FoodData Central). Protein plus fiber means fuller for longer.

Ancient grains and heart and gut health

  • Barley and oats are rich in beta-glucan. In a pooled analysis of trials, about 3 grams of barley beta-glucan a day lowered LDL cholesterol by about 7%.
  • Whole grains overall are linked to lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and early death.
  • Variety pays off. In the American Gut study, people who ate 30 or more different plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. Every new grain counts.

Ancient grains and brain health

The MIND diet calls for at least three servings of whole grains a day, and a Chicago study linked higher whole grain intake to slower cognitive decline. Ancient grains also deliver magnesium, B vitamins, and iron, nutrients your brain relies on, plus the steady blood sugar that protects memory over the long run.

A little history: Quinoa was so prized in the Andes that the Inca are said to have called it “the mother of all grains.” Farro fed ancient Rome. Millet farming in China dates back about 10,000 years. Amaranth was a staple and sacred food of the Aztecs.

How to Cook Ancient Grains

  1. Rinse in a fine-mesh strainer until the water runs clear.
  2. Use 2 to 3 cups of liquid per cup of grain (check the package).
  3. Bring to a boil, add the grain, and reduce the heat to low.
  4. Simmer covered until tender: quinoa about 15 minutes, farro about 30, hulled barley 45 to 60.
  5. Rest 5 minutes, then fluff with a fork.

More flavor: Dry-toast grains for 2 to 3 minutes first, cook them in low-sodium broth, add bay leaves or garlic, and finish with lemon juice or vinegar.

Portion guide: 1/4 cup dry grain makes about 3/4 cup cooked, a nice serving for steady energy.

How to store grains

  • Keep dry grains in airtight containers in a cool, dry pantry (or the fridge or freezer for longer storage), and use within about 6 months.
  • Check for pests, musty smells, or discoloration. When in doubt, throw it out.
  • Refrigerate cooked grains within 2 hours; they keep 4 to 5 days, or 3 months frozen. Reheat with a splash of broth.

Unlike processed foods, real whole grains can spoil, and that’s actually a good sign.

Safety check: If you have celiac disease or gluten sensitivity, farro, barley, spelt, einkorn, emmer, and Kamut all contain gluten and are not safe, despite claims that “ancient wheat” is easier to digest. Choose quinoa, amaranth, millet, buckwheat, wild rice, teff, sorghum, and certified gluten-free oats (Celiac Disease Foundation). If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, ancient grains may lower your blood sugar readings, so keep checking your levels and talk with your physician.

What Ancient Grains Do for Your Body

  • Protein power: Complete-protein grains plus beans make meatless meals truly satisfying.
  • Minerals: Magnesium (needed for more than 300 enzyme reactions, including blood sugar control), iron, and zinc, all largely stripped from white flour.
  • Natural B vitamins, right where nature put them in the bran and germ.
  • Gentle blood sugar: intact grains digest more slowly than flour.
  • Gut food: fiber that becomes short-chain fatty acids.

Many people notice they stay satisfied much longer in week 1, better digestion by week 2, and steadier energy, natural portion control, and less boredom with healthy eating after that.

Recipe: Mediterranean Ancient Grain Bowl

Ingredients: 1 cup organic farro, rinsed (or quinoa for gluten-free); 3 cups low-sodium vegetable broth; 2 tablespoons extra virgin olive oil; 1 diced red bell pepper; 1 diced zucchini; 1 cup halved cherry tomatoes; 1/4 red onion, thinly sliced; 1/4 cup halved kalamata olives; 2 tablespoons lemon juice; 2 minced garlic cloves; 1/4 cup chopped fresh basil; 2 tablespoons tahini; and salt and pepper.

Instructions:

  1. Simmer the farro in broth 25 to 30 minutes until tender (about 15 for quinoa), then drain.
  2. Sauté the pepper and zucchini in 1 tablespoon oil for about 5 minutes, then add the tomatoes for 2 more.
  3. Whisk the remaining oil, lemon juice, garlic, and tahini.
  4. Toss the grain, vegetables, onion, olives, basil, and dressing. Season and serve warm or at room temperature.

Add chickpeas and a handful of cooked greens for a complete one-bowl meal. It keeps beautifully for lunches.

Your Next Move

You’re connecting with thousands of years of food wisdom. Try 2 or 3 new ancient grains this week, make one a regular, and count how many different plant foods you eat. Aim for 30.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are ancient grains?

Ancient grains are grains and seeds that have changed little over centuries, like quinoa, farro, barley, amaranth, millet, and buckwheat. They’re usually eaten as intact whole grains, so they keep all their fiber, protein, and minerals.

Are ancient grains good for weight loss?

Yes. They’re rich in protein and fiber, which keep you full, and whole grains in general improve calorie balance. A cup of cooked quinoa has about twice the protein and several times the fiber of white rice.

Which ancient grains are gluten-free?

Quinoa, amaranth, millet, buckwheat, wild rice, teff, and sorghum are naturally gluten-free. Farro, barley, spelt, einkorn, emmer, and Kamut contain gluten and aren’t safe for people with celiac disease.

Is quinoa a complete protein?

Yes. Quinoa contains all nine essential amino acids, as do amaranth and buckwheat, which is rare among plant foods.

Does modern wheat have more gluten than ancient wheat?

A USDA review of a century of data found no clear evidence that the gluten content of U.S. wheat has increased. The bigger health issue is refining wheat into white flour and ultra-processed foods.
