The Healthiest Cooking Oil: Why Extra Virgin Olive Oil Beats Refined Seed Oils

Why extra virgin olive oil is the healthiest everyday cooking oil, what the seed oil debate really says, and oil-light techniques that still taste great.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 8 min read

Extra virgin olive oil is the healthiest everyday cooking oil for most people. In the PREDIMED trial, a Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil cut heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular deaths by about 30%, and using more than half a tablespoon a day was linked to a 28% lower risk of dementia death. Despite myths about its smoke point, it’s one of the most heat-stable oils. Swap out refined oils like soybean, corn, and “vegetable” oil, and measure your oil, since every tablespoon has about 120 calories.

This isn’t about going fat-free. Healthy fats are essential. It’s about choosing the right fats, in the right amounts, and getting as many as possible from whole foods.

Key Takeaways

  • Refined seed oils are solvent-extracted and heat-refined, stripping out natural antioxidants.
  • Extra virgin olive oil is simply pressed olives, rich in protective polyphenols.
  • Olive oil was linked to 19% lower risk of death and 28% lower risk of dementia death in a 28-year Harvard study.
  • Extra virgin olive oil was one of the most stable oils in heating tests.
  • Every oil has about 120 calories per tablespoon, so measure 1 to 2 teaspoons per serving.
  • Don’t go fat-free: fat helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E, K and carotenoids.

How Refined Oils Are Made

  • Extraction: Most refined seed oils (soybean, corn, cottonseed, and most canola) are extracted with hexane, a petroleum-derived solvent. Nearly all of it is removed, but it’s a far cry from pressing an olive.
  • Refining, bleaching, and deodorizing: High heat strips out color, flavor, and odor, along with many natural antioxidants, and can form small amounts of trans fats.
  • The result: A cheap, shelf-stable, flavorless fat, which is why it’s the backbone of ultra-processed foods, fast food, and restaurant fryers.

Extra virgin olive oil, by contrast, is olives crushed and pressed without heat or chemicals. It keeps its polyphenols, which protect the oil (and you) from oxidation.

Are Seed Oils Bad for You?

The science is debated, and I want to be honest about it. The modern diet has far more omega-6 than omega-3. But in controlled trials, eating more omega-6 (linoleic acid) generally hasn’t raised inflammation markers, and large studies link higher blood linoleic acid to lower heart disease risk. On the other side, re-analyses of older trials raised questions about replacing saturated fat with omega-6 oils.

Where nearly everyone agrees: refined seed oils show up mostly in ultra-processed, fried, and fast foods, and oils reheated in fryers are bad news. Extra virgin olive oil has some of the strongest evidence of any food in nutrition science. So swapping refined oils for extra virgin olive oil is a win no matter which side of the debate you’re on.

One Bottle, Three Battles

Olive oil and weight

Every oil, even the healthiest, is pure fat at about 120 calories per tablespoon, and free-pouring can add hundreds of calories to a meal. That’s why measuring matters. The good news: in the PREDIMED trial, people on a Mediterranean diet with generous extra virgin olive oil didn’t gain weight. They lost a little and gained less belly fat than the low-fat group.

Olive oil and heart health

  • In PREDIMED, high-risk adults on a Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil had about 30% fewer heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular deaths than those on a low-fat diet.
  • In a Harvard study of more than 90,000 adults followed for 28 years, those who used more than half a tablespoon of olive oil a day had a 19% lower risk of dying during the study. Replacing margarine, butter, mayonnaise, or dairy fat with olive oil was linked to lower risk, too.

Olive oil and brain health

  • In the same Harvard cohort, people who used more than about half a tablespoon a day had a 28% lower risk of dying from dementia than those who rarely used it. Swapping just a teaspoon a day of margarine or mayonnaise for olive oil was linked to an 8 to 14% lower risk.
  • In a Spanish PREDIMED substudy, adults on the Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil scored better on cognitive tests after about six years than the low-fat group.

Can You Cook With Extra Virgin Olive Oil?

Yes. You may have heard its smoke point is too low. But in heating tests, extra virgin olive oil was one of the most stable cooking oils, producing fewer harmful byproducts than oils with higher smoke points, thanks to its polyphenols and heat-resistant monounsaturated fats. It’s well suited to everyday sautéing and roasting.

Any oil heated past its smoke point, or reheated over and over in restaurant fryers, breaks down into compounds like aldehydes and acrolein linked to inflammation in lab studies. That’s one more reason skipping fried and fast food matters.

Measure it: 1 to 2 teaspoons per serving is usually plenty for cooking. Use a spoon or refillable oil mister instead of pouring from the bottle.

How to buy good extra virgin olive oil

  • Look for “extra virgin,” not “light,” “pure,” or plain “olive oil.”
  • Choose a dark glass bottle or tin.
  • Check for a harvest date within the last 12 to 18 months.
  • Third-party seals (California Olive Oil Council, North American Olive Oil Association) are a plus.
  • Store it in a cool, dark cupboard, not next to the stove.
  • Scan bottled dressings, marinades, and sauces; that’s where refined oils hide.

Safety check: Don’t go fat-free. Vitamins A, D, E, and K and vegetable carotenoids need fat to be absorbed. People absorbed far more carotenoids from a salad with a fat-containing dressing than a fat-free one. Include some healthy fat at every meal. If you’re losing weight quickly, very-low-fat diets can raise gallstone risk, so talk with your physician if you’ve had gallstones. If your LDL is high, go easy on coconut oil and keep butter out.

Oil-Light and Oil-Free Cooking Techniques

Water or broth sautéing:

  1. Heat a stainless steel or ceramic pan over medium heat.
  2. Add 2 to 3 tablespoons of water or low-sodium broth.
  3. Add vegetables when it starts to steam, stirring and adding liquid as needed.
  4. Finish with a drizzle of olive oil for flavor and absorption.

Steam-sautéing: Sear vegetables in a hot, dry pan for 30 seconds, add a splash of liquid, cover briefly to steam, then uncover and season with herbs, spices, and lemon.

Roasting with little or no oil: Use parchment or silicone mats, toss vegetables with citrus, vinegar, herbs, and spices, or use just a teaspoon of olive oil per sheet pan.

Build rich flavor: caramelize onions slowly in broth, let mushrooms brown in their own juices, blend herb pastes with lemon, and add nutritional yeast or miso. For crispiness, use high-heat roasting, broiling, or an air fryer.

Get Most of Your Fat From Whole Foods

Avocados, nuts and seeds, olives (a handful; they’re salty), fatty fish, and small amounts of unsweetened coconut deliver fats packaged with fiber, protein, vitamins, and antioxidants.

At restaurants: Ask what oil the kitchen uses, request olive oil or “light on the oil,” get dressings on the side, and favor Mediterranean or farm-to-table spots.

Recipe: No-Oil Rainbow Roasted Vegetables

Vegetables: 2 bell peppers in strips, 2 zucchini in half-moons, 1 red onion in wedges, 2 cups halved Brussels sprouts, 2 cups cauliflower florets, 1 cubed sweet potato, and 4 minced garlic cloves.

Oil-free marinade: 1/4 cup balsamic vinegar, 2 tablespoons low-sodium vegetable broth, 1 tablespoon lemon juice, 2 teaspoons dried oregano, 1 teaspoon each dried thyme and smoked paprika, 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder, and salt and pepper.

Instructions:

  1. Preheat the oven to 425°F and whisk the marinade.
  2. Toss the vegetables and marinate 15 to 20 minutes.
  3. Spread on parchment-lined sheets in a single layer.
  4. Roast 25 to 30 minutes, stirring halfway, until tender and caramelized (golden, not charred).
  5. Drizzle with leftover marinade and garnish with herbs.

Add fat at the table: avocado, tahini, toasted seeds, or a light drizzle of extra virgin olive oil, so you absorb all those carotenoids. Toss leftovers with farro or quinoa and chickpeas for a grain bowl.

Your Next Move

You’re not giving up flavor. You’re upgrading it. Great olive oil tastes fruity, grassy, and peppery; refined oils taste like nothing. Host an olive oil tasting with friends.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the healthiest oil to cook with?

Extra virgin olive oil is the best everyday choice. It’s linked to lower heart disease, death, and dementia risk, and it’s one of the most heat-stable oils in testing. Avocado oil is a good option for occasional very-high-heat cooking.

Is it safe to cook with extra virgin olive oil?

Yes. Despite its reputation, extra virgin olive oil held up better than many oils with higher smoke points in heating tests, thanks to its polyphenols and monounsaturated fats. It’s great for sautéing and roasting.

Are seed oils unhealthy?

The evidence is debated. Controlled studies generally don’t show that omega-6 oils raise inflammation, and some link them to lower heart disease risk. But refined seed oils mostly appear in ultra-processed and fried foods, so replacing them with extra virgin olive oil is a clear upgrade.

Is coconut oil healthy?

Coconut oil is high in saturated fat and raises LDL cholesterol, so it shouldn’t be your everyday oil. Use it occasionally for baking or flavor.

How much olive oil should I use a day?

About 1 to 2 teaspoons per serving for cooking is plenty. In research, more than half a tablespoon a day was linked to lower risk of death and dementia death. Measure rather than free-pour, since each tablespoon has about 120 calories.
