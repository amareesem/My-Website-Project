Two New Additions to the Dementia Risk Factor List, and Two Simple Checkups That Help

Two new dementia risk factors, high LDL cholesterol and untreated vision loss, joined the 2024 list. See what that means and which checkups help.

Lori King, MD · 2026-11-12 · 6 min read

If dementia runs in your family, it can feel as if the story is already written. Dr. Lori King and Dr. Chris King know that feeling. Each of them lost a parent to dementia. That is why they built PEAK Cognitive Longevity. A family history can raise your risk. It does not decide your future.

In 2024, a major research group called the Lancet Commission updated its list of dementia risk factors that people and communities can work on. The list now has 14 items. Two of them are new: high LDL cholesterol in midlife, and untreated vision loss later in life. Of the known risk factors for dementia, more than 80% are modifiable, and the Lancet Commission estimates that addressing just 14 of them could prevent or delay up to 45% of dementia cases worldwide.

That number is a population estimate. It is not a promise for any one person. Still, it is hopeful news. A lot of the risk sits in things we can measure and work on, including two simple checkups you can ask about at your next visit.

What Changed on the 2024 List

The Lancet Commission studies which risk factors for dementia might be changed through health care, lifestyle, or public policy. Its earlier list already included things such as hearing loss, high blood pressure, smoking, physical inactivity, diabetes, and social isolation.

In 2024, the Commission added two more, based on large new studies:

  • Higher LDL cholesterol in midlife. LDL is often called "bad" cholesterol because, when it is high, it can contribute to fatty buildup in blood vessels. Your brain depends on a steady blood supply, so vessel health and brain health are closely linked.
  • Untreated vision loss in later life. When vision problems go unaddressed, daily life can get harder, and people may move less, socialize less, or have more trouble with thinking tasks that depend on seeing clearly.

At a population level, the Commission estimated that high midlife LDL cholesterol accounts for about 7% of dementia cases, and untreated vision loss in later life (the report also uses the term uncorrected vision impairment) accounts for about 2%. Those figures are rounded, group-level estimates, not your personal odds. They simply show why these two items were added to the list.

You can read a short overview of the 2024 update, including how it fits with earlier research, on PEAK's Evidence page.

Why Midlife Cholesterol Matters, Even in Your 30s and 40s

Brain changes can begin many years before memory symptoms show up. That is one reason the 30s, 40s, and 50s are part of the window, not "too early" to care. Midlife is when high LDL is now counted as a dementia risk factor.

You do not need a special test to start. A standard cholesterol blood test, the kind many doctors already order, includes LDL. If you have not had yours checked, or if it has been several years, ask for the number and what it means for you. If it is high, your clinician can talk with you about food, movement, and, when needed, medication. This post is not a treatment plan. It is a reminder to know your number.

Why Eye Exams Belong on a Brain-Health Checklist

Vision loss was added as a risk factor when it is untreated. That word matters. Many vision problems can be helped with glasses, cataract surgery, or other care. The action is not to worry about your eyes. It is to keep up with exams and to follow through if something is found.

The National Institute on Aging, in its guidance on cognitive health, includes managing high cholesterol and treating hearing and vision loss among the steps that support brain health as we age. Hearing is a story of its own, and we will come back to it in a later post. For now, if it has been a while since your last eye exam, putting one on the calendar is a simple, concrete step.

Two Checkups, One Mindset

If you are 30 or older, if a parent or grandparent had dementia, or if you simply want to stay sharp for the years ahead, you do not have to overhaul your life this week. You can start with information.

  1. Ask your clinician for your latest cholesterol results, especially LDL, and whether midlife heart health is on your plan.
  2. Make sure you are current on eye exams, and treat vision problems if they turn up.

These steps will not erase family history, and they do not guarantee a particular outcome. They do put two official, modifiable risk factors on your radar. Dementia is not your destiny. Knowing where you stand is a calm, practical place to begin.

Frequently Asked Questions

What new dementia risk factors were added in 2024?

The Lancet Commission added high LDL cholesterol in midlife and untreated vision loss in later life. That brought its list of risk factors people and communities can work on to 14.

Does the 45% figure mean I can prevent dementia?

No. Of the known risk factors for dementia, more than 80% are modifiable, and the Lancet Commission estimates that addressing just 14 of them could prevent or delay up to 45% of dementia cases worldwide. That is an estimate for populations, not a promise for any one person.

What is LDL cholesterol, in plain words?

LDL is a type of cholesterol in your blood, often called "bad" cholesterol. When it is high, it can contribute to buildup in blood vessels. A routine blood test can measure it.

How often should I get my eyes checked for brain health?

Follow your eye doctor's advice for your age and eye history. The important point from the 2024 update is not a new exam schedule. It is that untreated vision loss is now recognized as a dementia risk factor, so keeping up with exams and treatment matters.
