How to Quit Sugar: A Doctor's Guide to Cutting Added Sugar and Artificial Sweeteners

How to quit sugar for good: a doctor's step-by-step plan to cut added sugar and sweeteners, beat cravings, lose weight, and protect your brain.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 9 min read

To quit sugar, clear added sugars and artificial sweeteners out of your kitchen, read every label for hidden sugar names, and ride out a few days of cravings. For most people, cravings peak around day 3 and ease a lot by day 5 to 10. The payoff is big: cutting added sugar helps with weight loss, lowers the risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes, and protects your brain from the blood sugar swings linked to dementia.

Let’s have a heart-to-heart about the sweet habit that’s been sabotaging your health goals since forever. Yes, all of it, including the sugar hiding behind fancy names like “organic cane sugar” and “evaporated cane juice,” and the zero-calorie sweetener in your coffee. (Spoiler: zero calories doesn’t mean zero consequences.)

Before you panic and start googling “how to survive without cookies,” take a deep breath. This is easier than you think, and the science will make you want to do it.

Key Takeaways

  • The American Heart Association recommends no more than about 25 grams (6 teaspoons) of added sugar a day for most women and 36 grams (9 teaspoons) for most men.
  • People who got 25% or more of their calories from added sugar had nearly 3 times the risk of dying from heart disease.
  • Higher blood sugar is linked to higher dementia risk, even without diabetes.
  • Sugar, inflammation, and belly fat feed each other by jamming your insulin and leptin (“I’m full”) signals.
  • “Natural” sweeteners like stevia and erythritol aren’t a free pass. The goal is retraining your taste buds, not finding a cleverer substitute.
  • Cravings are real biology, not weak willpower, and they fade faster than you’d expect.

What Added Sugar and Artificial Sweeteners Do to Your Body

Added sugar hits your bloodstream like a tsunami. Insulin spikes, your pancreas works overtime, and when that happens day after day, your cells start hanging a “Do Not Disturb” sign. That’s the road to insulin resistance.

Artificial sweeteners are sugar’s evil twin. They deliver intense sweetness with no calories, kind of like ordering pizza and getting a picture of pizza. Some studies suggest they keep sweet cravings revved up, and in a carefully controlled 2022 study in Cell, two weeks of saccharin or sucralose changed gut bacteria and blood sugar responses in some people.

One Spoonful, Three Battles

Added sugar and weight gain

Added sugar is the easiest calorie in the world to overeat. It’s quick to swallow, doesn’t fill you up, and usually comes packaged with refined flour and fat in foods designed to make you want “just one more.”

When researchers pooled the results of controlled trials in the BMJ, people who cut back on sugars lost weight and people who added sugars gained weight. And the fructose half of table sugar heads to your liver, where too much of it gets turned into fat, including the deep belly fat that fuels inflammation.

Added sugar and heart disease

The average American eats well over the American Heart Association’s limit. In a national study that followed nearly 12,000 adults for about 15 years, people who got 25% or more of their calories from added sugar had nearly three times the risk of dying from heart disease compared with those who kept it under 10%. Excess added sugar is also linked to type 2 diabetes, high triglycerides, fatty liver disease, and high blood pressure.

Sugar and brain health

Some researchers call Alzheimer’s disease “type 3 diabetes.” Here’s why:

  • Higher blood sugar, higher dementia risk. A study in the New England Journal of Medicine linked higher average blood sugar to a higher risk of dementia, even in people without diabetes.
  • Insulin resistance reaches the brain. In middle-aged adults at risk for Alzheimer’s, more insulin resistance meant less fuel uptake in the brain’s memory regions, years before any symptoms.
  • Sugary diets and brain plaque. Cognitively healthy older adults who ate a higher-glycemic diet (lots of sugar and refined starch) had more amyloid, the sticky protein linked to Alzheimer’s, in their brains.

Your brain runs best on steady fuel. Sugar gives it a roller coaster.

The Sugar, Inflammation, and Weight Loss Cycle

Chronic, low-grade inflammation and extra body fat feed each other, and two key hormones get caught in the crossfire:

  • Leptin is your “I’m full, stop eating” hormone. It’s made by fat cells, so people carrying extra weight usually have plenty of it, but their brains stop hearing the message. That’s leptin resistance, and inflammation is believed to be part of the cause.
  • Insulin helps your cells use glucose for energy. Inflammatory messengers interfere with its signal, so your body pumps out more and more insulin, and high insulin tells your body to store fat, not burn it.

The vicious cycle: too much sugar → more liver and belly fat → more inflammation → insulin and leptin stop working well → more cravings and fat storage → more inflammation. Quitting added sugar is how you jump off the merry-go-round.

Are “Natural” Sweeteners Like Stevia and Erythritol Safe?

They’re not the free pass they seem to be:

  • The World Health Organization’s 2023 guideline, which advises against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, includes stevia, not just aspartame, sucralose, and saccharin.
  • Erythritol, a popular sugar alcohol, was linked in a 2023 Nature Medicine study to a higher risk of heart attack and stroke, and it made platelets more likely to clot in the lab.

These are associations, and the research is still evolving. That’s exactly the point: the goal isn’t a cleverer substitute. It’s retraining your taste buds so you don’t need one.

Why Sugar Cravings Feel So Strong

Sugar lights up your brain’s reward system and releases dopamine. In animal studies, rats given sugar on and off start to binge, crave, and show withdrawal-like signs when it’s taken away. Scientists still debate whether “addiction” is the right word for humans, but your cravings are real biology.

The food industry spends millions finding the exact “bliss point” of sweetness that keeps you coming back. You’re not weak. You’ve been outgunned by food scientists.

How to Quit Sugar: Your Step-by-Step Plan

Step 1: Purge the pantry

Clear out:

  • Candy (yes, even “organic” gummy bears)
  • Cookies, cakes, ice cream, and frozen yogurt
  • Sweetened yogurt and sweetened cereal
  • Granola bars (candy bars in workout clothes)
  • Flavored coffee creamers
  • “Sugar-free” products and anything with aspartame, sucralose, saccharin, or acesulfame potassium
  • For now, maple syrup, honey, and agave, too

Step 2: Learn sugar’s aliases

Watch labels for high fructose corn syrup, dextrose, maltodextrin, rice syrup, cane juice, and fruit juice concentrate, plus dozens of other sneaky names. A food-scanning app like Bobby Approved catches them for you.

Step 3: Hydrate and fill up on fiber

A common rule of thumb is about half your body weight in ounces of water a day (pale-yellow urine means you’re on track). Load up on leafy greens, whose fiber helps steady blood sugar.

Step 4: Expect a short withdrawal

  • Headaches: common in the first few days. Stay hydrated.
  • Mood swings: your reward system is recalibrating.
  • Cravings: for most people they peak around day 3 and ease a lot by day 5.
  • Fatigue: your body is adjusting to steadier fuel.

Safety check: If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea (like glipizide, glyburide, or glimepiride), cutting added sugar can lower your blood sugar quickly and raise your risk of lows. Check your levels more often, talk with your physician about your dose, and keep glucose tablets on hand to treat a low. That’s medicine, not cheating. If you’re on a fluid limit for heart failure or kidney disease, follow your physician’s limit instead of the half-your-body-weight rule.

What Happens When You Stop Eating Sugar

  • Blood sugar and insulin settle down, for steadier energy and a steadier mind.
  • Your liver unloads fat. In children with obesity, cutting fructose while keeping calories the same reduced liver fat in just over a week.
  • Triglycerides often come down.
  • Insulin and leptin start working better as inflammation cools and weight comes off.
  • Your taste buds reset. After people cut back on sugar for three months, the same sweet pudding tasted noticeably sweeter to them.

A typical timeline: Days 1 to 3 can feel cranky. By days 4 to 7, many people notice steadier energy, better sleep, less bloating, and clearer thinking. By week 2 and beyond, fruit tastes incredibly sweet, appetite regulates itself, and weight loss gets easier.

Recipe: “Sweet Dreams Are Made of Dates” Energy Balls

Ingredients: 1 cup pitted Medjool dates, 1 cup raw almonds, 2 tablespoons unsweetened cocoa powder, 1 tablespoon chia seeds, 1 teaspoon pure vanilla extract, and a pinch of sea salt.

Instructions:

  1. Soak the dates in warm water for 10 minutes if they’re hard.
  2. Pulse the almonds in a food processor into coarse crumbs.
  3. Add the dates and process until the mixture sticks together.
  4. Add the cocoa, chia, vanilla, and salt, and process into a sticky dough.
  5. Roll into 12 balls and refrigerate for 30 minutes.

Dates are whole fruit, so their sweetness comes with fiber and potassium, and the almonds and chia slow how fast that sugar hits your bloodstream. They’re still a concentrated sweet, so one or two is a treat, not a meal. If you have diabetes, check your blood sugar 1 to 2 hours after your first one.

How to Handle Social Sugar Pressure

  • “Just one bite won’t hurt!” “Thanks, but I’m on a medical protocol right now and feeling amazing!”
  • “You’re being too extreme!” “My blood sugar, blood pressure, and energy have never been better.”
  • “Life’s too short to skip dessert!” “Life’s also too short to spend it tired and sick.”

Your Next Move

You’re not giving up sweetness forever. You’re taking a break from artificial, inflammatory sweetness so your body remembers what natural sweetness tastes like. Before long, a ripe strawberry will taste like candy. The cravings pass. The energy, clarity, and metabolic improvements are yours to keep.

Late-night craving hack: brush your teeth, drink a big glass of water, and remember that your future self is sending you a thank-you note.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does sugar withdrawal last?

For most people, cravings peak around day 3 and ease a lot by day 5, with many feeling noticeably better by day 10. Headaches, crankiness, and fatigue are common in the first few days and usually pass quickly with good hydration.

How much added sugar is OK per day?

The American Heart Association recommends no more than about 25 grams (6 teaspoons) of added sugar a day for most women and 36 grams (9 teaspoons) for most men. The natural sugar in whole fruit doesn’t count toward that limit.

Is stevia or monk fruit a healthy sugar substitute?

The WHO’s 2023 guideline advises against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, and it includes stevia. Erythritol has been linked to higher heart attack and stroke risk. Research is still evolving, so the safest long-term approach is retraining your taste for less sweetness.

Can quitting sugar help you lose weight?

Yes. Controlled trials pooled in the BMJ found that people who reduced sugar lost weight and people who increased it gained weight. Cutting added sugar also calms inflammation and helps insulin and leptin, your fullness hormone, work properly again.

Is sugar bad for your brain?

High blood sugar is linked to higher dementia risk, even in people without diabetes, and high-glycemic diets are linked to more Alzheimer’s-related amyloid in the brain. Keeping blood sugar steady is one of the most practical ways to protect memory.
