Fried Food and Fast Food: What They Do to Your Weight, Heart, and Brain

Fried and fast food are linked to weight gain, heart attack, diabetes, and even anxiety and depression. What the research says and how to quit.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 8 min read

Fried food and fast food are among the most consistent troublemakers in nutrition research. People who ate fast food more than twice a week gained about 10 more pounds over 15 years. Eating the most fried food is linked to a 28% higher risk of heart attack and stroke, and eating it daily to about a 55% higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Frequent fried food is also linked to more anxiety and depression. Baking, roasting, and planning ahead make it easy to skip the drive-thru.

I can practically hear you: “First my cookies, then my cereal, and now my french fries?” Here’s the thing. Fried and fast food aren’t just “a little unhealthy,” and after all the progress you’ve made, it’s time to stop letting the drive-thru undo your hard work.

Key Takeaways

  • Fast food more than twice a week meant about 10 extra pounds and twice the rise in insulin resistance over 15 years.
  • The highest fried-food intake is linked to a 28% higher risk of major cardiovascular events.
  • Fried food 4 to 6 times a week is linked to a 39% higher diabetes risk; daily, about 55%.
  • Frying reuses oil that breaks down, and high-heat starches form acrylamide.
  • Fast food packaging can contain PFAS (“forever chemicals”), and fast food eaters have higher phthalate levels.
  • Baked “golden, not brown” wedges satisfy crispy cravings with far less oil and acrylamide.

What Happens When Food Meets the Fryer

  • Oil that’s cooked again and again. Restaurant fryers heat oil for hours or days. With each reuse, it breaks down into oxidation products, including aldehydes, linked to inflammation and cell damage in lab studies. Your food soaks it up.
  • Acrylamide. When starchy foods like potatoes are fried, baked, or roasted until very brown, acrylamide forms. The WHO’s cancer agency classifies it as “probably carcinogenic to humans,” based mainly on animal studies. French fries and potato chips are among the biggest sources in the American diet.
  • AGEs (advanced glycation end products). High-heat frying and grilling create AGEs, which are linked to oxidative stress and inflammation. Small studies suggest lowering them can reduce inflammatory markers.
  • Trans fats are mostly gone. The FDA removed artificial trans fats from the U.S. food supply, fully in effect by 2020. But fried food still earns its reputation, as the research below shows.

Are seed oils bad for you?

This is debated. The modern diet does have far more omega-6 than omega-3. But in controlled studies, eating more omega-6 (linoleic acid) generally hasn’t raised inflammation markers, and some large studies link it to lower heart disease risk. Where there is strong agreement: oil heated over and over in a fryer, and the fried and ultra-processed foods it ends up in, are bad news. Meanwhile, keep reaching for omega-3-rich foods like walnuts, flax, chia, and fish.

One Fry, Three Battles

Fast food and weight gain

In the 15-year CARDIA study of more than 3,000 young adults, those who ate fast food more than twice a week gained about 10 more pounds than those who ate it less than once a week, and had twice the increase in insulin resistance. A large order of fries can top 450 calories, and fast food is engineered to be eaten quickly, in big portions, with a sugary drink.

Fried food, heart disease, and diabetes

  • When researchers pooled studies of more than half a million people, those who ate the most fried food had about a 28% higher risk of heart attack, stroke, and other major cardiovascular events.
  • In two large Harvard studies, eating fried food 4 to 6 times a week was linked to about a 39% higher risk of type 2 diabetes, and eating it daily to about a 55% higher risk.
  • A single fast food combo meal can blow past the entire day’s recommended sodium limit of 2,300 mg.

Fried food and brain health

Every 10% increase in ultra-processed food was linked to a 25% higher dementia risk, and fast food is ultra-processed food served hot (see ultra-processed foods and dementia). Fried food may affect mood, too. In a study of more than 140,000 UK adults, frequent fried food eaters, especially fans of fried potatoes, had a 12% higher risk of anxiety and a 7% higher risk of depression. The same team found a compound formed in fried food triggered brain inflammation in zebrafish.

Why Fast Food Is So Hard to Resist

  • Engineered cravings: food scientists fine-tune salt, sugar, fat, and flavorings to hit your brain’s “bliss point” and override fullness.
  • Additives: long lists of preservatives, emulsifiers, dough conditioners, and flavorings.
  • Hidden sugar in buns, sauces, dressings, and marinades.
  • Sodium overload that raises blood pressure and causes water retention.
  • Packaging chemicals: grease-resistant wrappers have been found to contain PFAS, and people who eat more fast food tend to have higher phthalate levels.

How Fried and Fast Food Fight Your Metabolism

  1. Calorie overload you don’t notice: a whole day’s calories in one sitting.
  2. Insulin spikes from refined grains, sugar, and fat together.
  3. Inflammation that jams insulin and leptin, the signals that manage hunger and fat storage.
  4. Stressed blood vessels: studies suggest a single high-fat fast food meal can temporarily impair your blood vessels’ ability to relax for several hours.

How to Stop Eating Fried Food and Fast Food

Clear out: fries and chips, fried chicken and fish, donuts, onion rings and fried appetizers, fried rice and noodles, tempura, and frozen pre-fried foods like many fries, nuggets, and breaded fish.

Watch for: salads with fried toppings (crispy chicken, tortilla strips, wonton crisps), “grilled” items brushed with butter or sugary glazes, and “baked, not fried” snacks that are still ultra-processed.

Plan ahead:

  1. Batch-cook proteins and vegetables on Sunday.
  2. Pre-cut vegetables and make big batches of healthy dips.
  3. Keep an emergency kit: pre-washed greens, canned wild salmon or sardines, avocados, nuts and seeds, and pre-cooked quinoa or brown rice.
  4. Find 2 or 3 local restaurants that grill, bake, roast, or steam with whole ingredients.

Eating out without fried food

Mediterranean and farm-to-table restaurants are great bets. Ask:

  • “Is this fried or pre-fried?”
  • “Can you prepare this with olive oil instead?”
  • “Can I substitute vegetables for the fries?”
  • “Can I get the sauce on the side?”

Healthy swaps for crispy cravings

Roasted vegetables, baked root vegetable chips (golden, not brown), toasted nuts and seeds, baked kale chips, and air-fried vegetables with a light mist of oil. For rich flavor: avocado dips, nut butters, tahini, hummus, and cashew cream.

Safety check: Cutting fast food can dramatically lower your sodium and your blood pressure. If you take blood pressure medication, check your pressure at home, watch for dizziness when standing, and talk with your physician about your dose. If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, dropping fast food’s refined carbs and sugar can lower your blood sugar, so check your levels more often.

What Happens When You Quit Fast Food

  • Fewer hidden calories, and weight loss without counting
  • Lower sodium and blood pressure, often within a couple of weeks
  • Healthier blood vessels and less inflammation
  • Less exposure to acrylamide and packaging chemicals

Many people feel “lighter” and less sluggish in week 1, notice less bloating and clearer skin by week 2, and see steadier energy, better sleep, lower blood pressure, and more consistent weight loss after that. Cravings for the salt-fat-sugar hit can be strong for the first 5 days. That’s your brain’s reward system talking, not your body needing nutrition.

Recipe: “Crispy” Baked Sweet Potato Wedges

Ingredients: 3 large organic sweet potatoes, 2 tablespoons avocado oil, 1 teaspoon each smoked paprika, garlic powder, and onion powder, 1/2 teaspoon cumin, 1/2 teaspoon sea salt, 1/4 teaspoon black pepper, and 1/4 teaspoon cayenne (optional).

Instructions:

  1. Preheat the oven to 425°F.
  2. Cut the sweet potatoes into even wedges, skin on.
  3. Soak in cold water for 30 minutes to remove surface starch for crispier wedges and less acrylamide.
  4. Pat completely dry, then toss with oil and seasonings.
  5. Spread in a single layer on a parchment-lined sheet (crowding makes them steam).
  6. Bake 25 to 30 minutes, flipping halfway. Pull them when golden with crispy edges, not dark brown.

The pre-soak and “golden, not brown” rule follow FDA tips for reducing acrylamide at home. For a “loaded” version, top with black beans, cashew cream, cilantro, and lime.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is fried food bad for you?

Fried food is calorie-dense, often cooked in oil that's been reused and broken down, and can contain acrylamide and AGEs. The highest fried-food intake is linked to a 28% higher risk of heart attack and stroke and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes.

How much weight can you gain from fast food?

In the 15-year CARDIA study, young adults who ate fast food more than twice a week gained about 10 more pounds than those who ate it less than once a week, and had twice the increase in insulin resistance.

Are seed oils bad for you?

The science is debated. Controlled studies generally haven't found that omega-6 fats raise inflammation, and some link them to lower heart disease risk. The clearer problem is oil that's reused in fryers and the fried, ultra-processed foods it ends up in.

Is air-fried food healthy?

Air frying uses far less oil than deep frying, so it's a much better option for crispy vegetables. Cook starchy foods until golden rather than dark brown to keep acrylamide low, and remember that air-fried processed foods are still processed.

What can I eat instead of fast food when I'm busy?

Keep an emergency kit of pre-washed greens, canned wild salmon or sardines, avocados, nuts, and pre-cooked quinoa or brown rice. Batch-cook proteins and roasted vegetables on Sunday, and choose restaurants that grill, bake, or roast.
