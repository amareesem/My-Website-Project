Anti-Inflammatory Herbs and Spices: The Health Benefits of Turmeric, Ginger, Garlic, and More

The health benefits of turmeric, ginger, garlic, cinnamon, and more, what the research really shows, how to avoid lead in spices, and a golden milk recipe.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 8 min read

Herbs and spices are among the most antioxidant-rich foods ever measured, gram for gram. Some have solid research behind them: ginger eases nausea, garlic supplements lower blood pressure by about 8 points, and about a tablespoon of mixed herbs and spices a day lowered 24-hour blood pressure in a controlled trial. Turmeric’s curcumin shows promise for joints and memory, mostly at supplement doses. Their biggest everyday benefit is making healthy food taste so good you stick with it. Buy from brands that test for heavy metals.

These aren’t just flavor additions. Many have been used in traditional medicine for thousands of years, and modern science is catching up on why.

Key Takeaways

  • About 6 1/2 grams (roughly a tablespoon) of mixed herbs and spices a day lowered 24-hour blood pressure.
  • Garlic supplements lowered systolic blood pressure by roughly 8 points in people with hypertension.
  • Curcumin is poorly absorbed alone; black pepper and a little fat help.
  • An 18-month trial found a well-absorbed curcumin supplement improved memory scores.
  • About one-third of spices Consumer Reports tested had concerning heavy metal levels, and organic didn’t guarantee lower levels.
  • Use Ceylon cinnamon if you eat cinnamon daily.

Health Benefits of Common Spices: What the Research Says

  • Turmeric (curcumin): Strongly anti-inflammatory in the lab. In some trials, concentrated curcumin extracts eased knee arthritis pain about as well as ibuprofen. The catch: those were supplement doses far higher than cooking provides.
  • Ginger: One of the best-studied spices. It reliably helps with nausea from pregnancy, motion sickness, and chemotherapy, and may modestly ease muscle soreness and arthritis pain.
  • Garlic: In people with high blood pressure, garlic supplements, especially aged garlic extract, lowered systolic pressure by roughly 8 points. Crush it and let it sit 10 minutes before cooking to help form its active compounds.
  • Cinnamon: Some studies show it modestly lowers fasting blood sugar, though results are mixed. It adds sweetness without sugar, a win on its own.

One Pinch, Three Battles

Spices and weight loss

Spices won’t melt fat, but they make healthy food taste amazing, and that’s how people stick with it. Bold flavor lets you cut salt, sugar, and fat without feeling deprived. And capsaicin, the compound that makes chili peppers hot, gives your metabolism a small bump, roughly 50 extra calories a day in some studies, and may help you feel full sooner.

Spices, blood pressure, and inflammation

In a Penn State feeding study, adults at risk for heart disease who ate about 6 1/2 grams (roughly a tablespoon) of mixed herbs and spices a day had lower 24-hour blood pressure than when they ate the same diet with just a pinch. In another study, adding a spice blend to a rich meal reduced the inflammatory response afterward.

Spices and brain health

In an 18-month UCLA trial, adults ages 51 to 84 who took a well-absorbed curcumin supplement improved their memory scores compared with placebo, and brain scans suggested fewer Alzheimer’s-linked protein deposits in memory areas. It was a small study (40 people) using a supplement, not the turmeric in your curry, but it’s an intriguing hint. Small trials of sage and rosemary have also shown short-term boosts in memory and alertness.

How to Get More From Turmeric

Curcumin on its own is poorly absorbed. Two tricks help:

  1. Add black pepper. Its piperine blocks curcumin’s breakdown. In one study at supplement doses, piperine boosted blood curcumin by about 2,000%.
  2. Add a little fat. Curcumin dissolves in fat, so cook it in olive oil or pair it with coconut milk or nuts.

Are Your Spices Safe? Heavy Metals and Labels

The real concern is heavy metals. In 2021, Consumer Reports tested 126 herb and spice products and found about one-third had lead, arsenic, and cadmium levels high enough to be concerning, especially for children who eat them regularly. Every oregano and thyme product they tested raised concerns. Organic products weren’t consistently lower, because organic standards don’t require heavy metal testing. In 2023 and 2024, the FDA also recalled lead-contaminated cinnamon products, including cinnamon applesauce pouches that poisoned hundreds of children.

What to do:

  • Buy from established brands that test for heavy metals and share results.
  • Be cautious with spices bought abroad or from bulk bins with unknown sources.
  • Don’t pick turmeric by how bright it looks. Some imported turmeric has been illegally colored with lead chromate.
  • Grow and dry your own oregano and thyme.
  • Rotate your spices so none dominates.

Why organic still matters: Organic spices can’t be irradiated or fumigated with ethylene oxide, a sterilizing gas linked to cancer at high exposures. Irradiation at approved levels is considered safe, but if you’d rather skip it, organic is your answer.

How to store spices

  • Keep them in airtight glass jars in a cool, dark cupboard, not above the stove.
  • Whole spices last about 2 to 3 years; ground spices and dried herbs are best within 1 to 2.
  • Replace anything that smells musty, flat, or dusty.
  • Never shake spices over a steaming pot or use a wet spoon.

Aim to use at least 3 different herbs or spices every day.

Safety check: Cooking amounts are safe for almost everyone; these concerns apply mainly to supplements and very large amounts. Blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, clopidogrel, aspirin): turmeric, garlic, ginger, and fenugreek supplements may raise bleeding risk, so talk with your physician first and before surgery. Diabetes medications: cinnamon, fenugreek, and curcumin supplements may lower blood sugar. Turmeric supplements: rare liver injury has been reported, especially with high-dose or piperine-enhanced products; skip them if you have gallstones or liver disease unless your physician approves. Cinnamon: everyday cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which can harm the liver in large amounts, so use Ceylon if you eat it daily. Star anise: use only Chinese star anise (Japanese is toxic), and never give star anise tea to infants.

Smart Spice Pairings

  • Turmeric + black pepper + a little fat: the classic absorption trio.
  • Tomatoes + olive oil + oregano: oil and cooking boost lycopene absorption.
  • Garlic: crush, wait 10 minutes, then cook.
  • Cook herbs in olive oil: many of their compounds are fat-soluble.
  • Hardy vs. tender herbs: add rosemary, thyme, and bay early; basil, cilantro, and parsley at the end.

Three easy spice blends

  • Golden blend: 2 tablespoons turmeric, 1 tablespoon ginger, 1 teaspoon black pepper, 1 teaspoon Ceylon cinnamon. Use on roasted vegetables and soups.
  • Digestive blend: 2 tablespoons fennel seeds, 1 tablespoon coriander seeds, 1 teaspoon cardamom pods, 1 teaspoon ground ginger. Grind and steep as an after-meal tea.
  • Mediterranean blend: 2 tablespoons oregano, 1 tablespoon each thyme and rosemary, 1 teaspoon garlic powder. Great on vegetables, beans, fish, and chicken.

Recipe: Golden Milk (Turmeric Latte)

Ingredients: 1 cup unsweetened plant milk (light coconut, almond, or oat), 1 teaspoon organic turmeric, 1/2 teaspoon ground ginger (or 1 teaspoon fresh grated), 1/4 teaspoon Ceylon cinnamon, a pinch each of black pepper, cardamom (optional), and sea salt.

Instructions: Warm the milk over medium-low heat, whisk in the spices, and simmer 3 to 5 minutes, whisking often. Strain if you like, add salt to taste, and serve warm.

The pepper and the fat in coconut milk help you absorb curcumin (with a lower-fat milk, have a few nuts on the side). The cinnamon and cardamom bring sweetness without sugar. Many people enjoy it as a caffeine-free evening ritual. Blend it for 20 seconds for a frothy latte, or roast cauliflower with turmeric, cumin, and pepper at 425°F.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best anti-inflammatory spices?

Turmeric, ginger, garlic, cinnamon, rosemary, and oregano are among the best studied. Herbs and spices are some of the most antioxidant-rich foods measured, and adding a spice blend to meals has reduced post-meal inflammation in small studies.

How much turmeric should I take a day?

For cooking, a teaspoon or so a day with black pepper and a little fat is a reasonable, safe amount. Supplement doses used in studies are much higher; talk with your physician before taking curcumin supplements, especially if you take blood thinners or have liver or gallbladder disease.

Does black pepper really help turmeric absorption?

Yes. Piperine in black pepper blocks the breakdown of curcumin. In one study at supplement doses, it raised blood curcumin levels by about 2,000%. Pairing turmeric with a little fat also helps.

Do spices contain lead?

Some do. Consumer Reports found concerning levels of lead, arsenic, or cadmium in about one-third of tested herbs and spices, and organic wasn't consistently lower. Buy from brands that test for heavy metals and be cautious with spices of unknown origin.

What's the difference between Ceylon and cassia cinnamon?

Cassia, the most common type, contains coumarin, which can harm the liver in large amounts. Ceylon cinnamon has very little, so it's the better choice if you use cinnamon every day.
