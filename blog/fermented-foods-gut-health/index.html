Fermented Foods for Gut Health: How Sauerkraut, Kimchi, and More Support Your Weight, Immunity, and Brain

Fermented foods boosted gut microbe diversity and cut 19 inflammatory markers in a Stanford trial. The best ones, how to start, and homemade sauerkraut.

Lori King, MD · 2026-09-01 · 8 min read

Fermented foods like sauerkraut, kimchi, tempeh, miso, water kefir, and kombucha deliver live beneficial microbes that support your gut microbiome. In a Stanford trial, eating about six servings of fermented foods a day for 10 weeks increased gut microbe diversity and lowered 19 inflammatory proteins in the blood. Because chronic inflammation drives weight gain, chronic disease, and cognitive decline, a healthier gut may help all three. Start with 1 to 2 tablespoons a day and choose refrigerated, unpasteurized ferments.

Meet the unsung heroes of your health: the trillions of microbes living in your gut. Fermented foods are nature’s original probiotic foods, and humans have been making them for thousands of years.

Key Takeaways

  • Your body has roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, about 38 trillion, mostly in your colon.
  • A fermented-food diet increased gut microbe diversity and lowered 19 inflammatory markers in 10 weeks.
  • About 70% of your immune cells live in and around your gut.
  • Real fermented foods are refrigerated and unpasteurized, with no vinegar.
  • Start small (1 to 2 tablespoons a day) to avoid gas and bloating.
  • Fermented foods are high in tyramine, a concern if you take an MAOI antidepressant.

What Is the Gut Microbiome?

  • You’re about half microbe. Scientists estimate your body holds roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, around 38 trillion, mostly in your colon. They influence digestion, immunity, and metabolism.
  • The gut-brain axis. About 90% of your body’s serotonin is made in your gut, with gut bacteria helping regulate how much. Most of it works right in the gut, but your gut and brain are in constant conversation through the vagus nerve, immune signals, and compounds your bacteria make.
  • Immune headquarters. Roughly 70% of your immune cells live in and around your gut, where microbes help train them to tell friend from foe.

Cutting added sugar, ultra-processed foods, and fried foods, and eating more fiber, already feeds your good bacteria. Fermented foods send in reinforcements.

One Forkful, Three Battles

Fermented foods and weight

Gut bacteria turn fiber into short-chain fatty acids that help regulate appetite and blood sugar, and people with more diverse microbiomes tend to have healthier weights. Fermented foods are low in calories and big on flavor. In a large Korean study, men who ate one to three servings of kimchi a day were less likely to be obese, though very high intakes weren’t better, likely because of the salt and rice that often come with it. Moderation wins.

Fermented foods and inflammation

This is the study that got me excited. At Stanford, healthy adults ate either a high-fermented-food diet (about six servings a day of foods like yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, and kombucha) or a high-fiber diet for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group increased the diversity of their gut microbes and lowered 19 inflammatory proteins, including interleukin-6, which is linked to diabetes, heart disease, and chronic stress. The high-fiber group didn’t see those changes in 10 weeks. That’s why pairing fiber (to feed the bacteria you have) with fermented foods (to add new ones) makes sense.

Gut health and brain health

Chronic inflammation is strongly linked to cognitive decline and dementia, so calming it is one way gut health may support the brain. People with depression and Alzheimer’s tend to have different gut bacteria than healthy people, and small trials of probiotics have shown modest mood improvements and, in one small trial, better memory scores in people with Alzheimer’s. The research is early, but it’s one of the most exciting frontiers in brain health.

Fermented Foods vs. Probiotic Supplements

Fermented foods deliver live microbes in their natural food “home,” along with fiber and nutrients, like giving bacteria a fully furnished apartment instead of an empty room. Different ferments contain different microbes, so variety builds a more diverse, resilient microbiome. Supplements have their place for specific conditions, but for everyday gut health, food comes first.

How to Tell Real Fermented Foods From Imposters

  • True fermentation uses salt and time (real sauerkraut, kimchi, naturally fermented pickles).
  • Vinegar-pickled foods are tasty but have no live cultures (most shelf-stable pickles).
  • Pasteurized “fermented” foods have been heat-treated, killing the bacteria.

Look for: the refrigerated section, a short ingredient list (vegetables, salt, spices), no vinegar, “unpasteurized,” “raw,” or “live cultures” on the label, and often a little fizz. Scan labels for added sugar and preservatives.

How to Start Eating Fermented Foods

  1. Start small: 1 to 2 tablespoons of sauerkraut or kimchi, or a small glass of water kefir, a day.
  2. Go slow: If you notice gas or bloating, drop to a teaspoon and build up over a couple of weeks.
  3. Diversify: Try 3 different fermented foods this week, working up to a serving or two daily.
  4. Pair with fiber: Keep eating beans, greens, and whole grains to feed the new arrivals.

Safety check: High blood pressure, heart failure, or kidney disease: sauerkraut, kimchi, and miso are high in sodium, so rinse sauerkraut lightly and keep portions small. MAOI antidepressants (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline) or linezolid: sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, soy sauce, and tempeh are high in tyramine, which can cause a dangerous blood pressure spike; talk with your physician first. Weakened immune system: ask your physician before eating unpasteurized, live-culture foods. Pregnancy: skip unpasteurized homemade kombucha. Histamine intolerance: fermented foods are high in histamine and may trigger headaches or flushing.

How to Make Sauerkraut at Home

A $3 head of cabbage can make weeks of sauerkraut that would cost $15 to $30 to buy.

  1. Shred 2 lbs organic cabbage.
  2. Mix with 1 tablespoon sea salt (about 2% of the cabbage’s weight).
  3. Massage until the juices release.
  4. Pack tightly into a clean jar, pressing the cabbage below the brine.
  5. Ferment at room temperature 1 to 4 weeks, tasting after the first week.
  6. Refrigerate when it’s as sour as you like.

Safety rules: Use about 2% salt by weight, keep vegetables submerged, and use clean equipment. Good ferments smell pleasantly sour. Throw out any batch that’s slimy, smells rotten, or has fuzzy or colored mold (pink, black, or green); don’t scrape it off. Leave kombucha to the pros if you’re new, since it’s more prone to contamination. For more guidance, see the National Center for Home Food Preservation.

Recipe: “Microbiome Paradise” Bowl

Bowl: 2 cups mixed greens, 1/2 cup unpasteurized sauerkraut, 1/4 cup kimchi, 1 cup cooked quinoa (cooled to room temperature), 1/2 sliced avocado, 1/4 cup diced cucumber, 2 tablespoons hemp seeds, 1 tablespoon pumpkin seeds, and fresh cilantro, parsley, and chives.

Dressing: 2 tablespoons tahini, 1 tablespoon lemon juice, 1 tablespoon water kefir (or more lemon), 1 teaspoon miso, 1 minced garlic clove, and water to thin.

Instructions: Layer the greens and room-temperature quinoa (very hot food can kill live cultures), top with the sauerkraut, kimchi, avocado, cucumber, and seeds, drizzle with dressing, and garnish with herbs. Sauerkraut, kimchi, and miso are salty, so skip added salt.

More ideas: Stir miso into soup after it’s off the heat, or top eggs with a forkful of kimchi.

What to Expect

A little gas in week 1 is normal as your microbiome adjusts; starting small keeps it comfortable. By week 2, many people notice better regularity and less bloating. After that, meals taste more interesting, digestion steadies, and some people notice a brighter mood and fewer colds. Everyone’s microbiome is unique.

Don’t like the sour taste? Start with milder miso soup, water kefir, or a spoonful of sauerkraut mixed into a salad. Your taste buds adjust.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best fermented foods for gut health?

Unpasteurized sauerkraut, kimchi, tempeh, miso, water or coconut kefir, and low-sugar kombucha are great choices. Different ferments contain different microbes, so eating a variety supports a more diverse microbiome.

How much fermented food should I eat a day?

Start with 1 to 2 tablespoons a day and work up to a serving or two daily. In the Stanford study, about six servings a day for 10 weeks increased microbial diversity and lowered inflammation.

Are fermented foods better than probiotic supplements?

For everyday gut health, fermented foods are a great first choice because they deliver live microbes along with fiber and nutrients. Probiotic supplements can be helpful for specific medical conditions under your physician's guidance.

Is store-bought sauerkraut probiotic?

Only if it's refrigerated, unpasteurized, and made without vinegar. Shelf-stable sauerkraut is usually pasteurized, which kills the live bacteria.

Can fermented foods help with weight loss?

They may support it indirectly. Fermented foods improve microbiome diversity and lower inflammation, and a Korean study linked one to three servings of kimchi a day to lower obesity in men. They're also low in calories and add big flavor to vegetables and grains.
